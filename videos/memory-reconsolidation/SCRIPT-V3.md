# YOUR MEMORY IS NOT THE ORIGINAL — v3, the Derek rewrite

**~5:30.** The comedy now comes from **character and commitment**, not props.

## Why v1 and v2 weren't funny

Both were *ironic* — things slightly wrong, described dryly. Wry is tasteful and
tasteful is the enemy. Every joke was a prop gag; not one came from a person.
Nobody in the film wanted anything. And the narrator was calm rather than funny,
which is not what deadpan means — deadpan is *funny lines delivered flat*.

## The three fixes

**1. Derek.** The archive gets one recurring employee with a personality:
total confidence, no competence, faintly wounded when questioned. He is not
malicious. He is doing his best, and his best is Spain, probably. Every archive
beat is Derek. No interchangeable staff.

**2. The protagonist is specifically petty**, not generally defensive. They are
the person who says *"I'm not saying I'm right, I'm saying I remember it
correctly."* They never raise their voice. They are always losing.

**3. Commit past comfortable.** The escalation rule: when a mistake is pointed
out, Derek does not fix it — **he adds another one.** Wrong wall → second wrong
wall. Wrong dog → more dog.

## Frame rules

- **Close.** Mid and reaction shots. Three wide shots in the whole film.
- **THE FORMULA, verbatim:** recognisable setup, impossible cartoon payoff.
- **Derek is on screen for every archive beat**, same character, same face.
- 20% physics · 6% flourish.

## VO

> You and your partner are having the argument about the holiday.
>
> You're not angry. You're being *reasonable*. You've just said the words "I'm
> not saying I'm right, I'm saying I remember it correctly," which is a sentence
> that has never once been true.
>
> Here is what is happening while you say it.
>
> Somewhere behind your eyes, a man called Derek has been handed a job. Derek
> does not have your holiday. **Nobody** has your holiday. What Derek has is a
> note that says *Spain, probably*, and about eleven seconds.
>
> Derek is going to build Spain.
>
> This is the part people get wrong about memory. You think you're opening a
> file. There is no file. There has never been a file. There is Derek, and a
> shelf, and a deadline.
>
> Take your childhood kitchen. Yellow walls. The red mug by the sink. Blue
> curtains.
>
> There were no blue curtains.
>
> Derek added those in twenty fourteen. He thought the window looked bare. He
> was not asked.
>
> And now you want to argue with me about your own kitchen — which, I want to be
> clear, is the correct instinct. It's just that you're arguing on Derek's
> behalf and you don't know it.
>
> Here's how the job actually goes.
>
> Derek does not keep your kitchen. Derek keeps *notes* about your kitchen. So
> when you ask for it — and you did, just now, mid-argument, with your hand up —
> he builds it. At speed. From whatever is within reach.
>
> Wall's too short? Derek is aware. Derek has decided that's fine.
>
> Missing a detail? There's a box for that. Everything in the box is
> **approximately** correct, and it has never once been checked.
>
> And here is where it gets expensive. Because Derek doesn't only use what he
> had at the time. Derek uses whatever is lying around **now**.
>
> In nineteen seventy-four, Elizabeth Loftus showed people a film of a car
> crash. She asked one group how fast the cars were going when they **hit** each
> other. She asked another group how fast when they **smashed**.
>
> Same film. The smashed group remembered it faster. And a week later, more of
> them remembered broken glass.
>
> There was no broken glass. The question put it there. One word. One word got
> in, and Derek filed it.
>
> And you'll want to tell me you'd have spotted it. There's a review from twenty
> twenty-four on precisely that — whether being cleverer protects you. It helps.
> It genuinely helps. Nobody in it was immune. Not one.
>
> Now. The story your family tells about you at Christmas.
>
> Do you remember the day? Or do you remember your mum telling it?
>
> You cannot tell from in here. They come back in the same handwriting. Derek
> writes everything in the same handwriting.
>
> Which brings us to the part that made all this famous, and the part where I
> have to be careful.
>
> Two thousand and seven. Learn a list of objects on Monday. On Tuesday, get a
> small reminder of Monday — then learn a second list. On Wednesday, recall the
> first one.
>
> The people who got the reminder put Tuesday's objects into Monday's list. Not
> the other way round. The reminder did it.
>
> There's a proposed explanation. It's called reconsolidation. The idea is that
> pulling a memory up leaves it briefly unsettled — open — before it sets again.
> And while it's open, whatever's nearby can wander in.
>
> That's the idea. Hold on to the word *proposed*. I'll come back for it.
>
> Because if it's right, then what Derek hands you isn't just what you see. It's
> what goes back on the shelf.
>
> The copy becomes the master. Same box. Same label. Derek has never in his life
> written the word "approximately" on anything.
>
> So you ask him for the original.
>
> And Derek, who has been doing this job for thirty-one years, discovers a
> sudden and passionate interest in the ceiling.
>
> Now — the honest part, and it is a real one.
>
> The behaviour is solid. Remind people, hand them something new, and the new
> thing gets in. A meta-analysis in twenty seventeen found that reliably.
>
> The mechanism is not solid. In twenty sixteen, a team ran seven experiments
> trying to reproduce a well-known human demonstration of it. They found
> nothing. Seven times.
>
> The reply was that the conditions weren't right. And the reply to *that* was:
> then tell us what the conditions are, so we can test those as well.
>
> A major review in twenty eighteen called it a viable but hotly contested
> explanation. That is still exactly where it sits.
>
> There's also a plainer story that might account for the same results. Not that
> the memory changed — that you got confused about where a detail came from.
> Nobody has ruled it out.
>
> And while we're here: getting a whole invented memory into somebody is harder
> than people say. Not impossible. Harder. And even that number is argued about.
>
> So. Back to the holiday.
>
> You're not lying. They're not lying. You are both reading out, with total
> confidence, a thing that Derek made this morning.
>
> Your memory isn't useless because it changes. It changes because it was built
> to keep the past useful — and useful was always going to beat exact.
>
> Go and ask them about the curtains.
>
> Derek's not going to like it.

## The gags — Derek carries all of them

| Beat | The joke |
|---|---|
| The argument | Close on two faces mid-argument. Between them, *two* small workshops building two completely different living rooms. Neither notices |
| No file | Protagonist yanks a filing drawer with total confidence. The drawer keeps coming — six feet of empty drawer — and there is nothing in it |
| Derek's intro | Derek, mid-shot, holding one tiny note, looking at a vast empty stage. Total calm |
| Blue curtains | Protagonist gesturing proudly at the curtains. They are visibly still wet. Derek is frozen mid-brushstroke, making eye contact with the viewer |
| The short wall | The wall is two feet short. Protagonist points at it. **Derek adds a second short wall.** |
| The PROBABLY box | Derek reaches in without looking and pulls out a completely different person's face. Puts it on the memory. Moves on |
| "Smashed" | The word hits the protagonist's head like a punch and snaps it back. Red glass sprays into a memory that was clean a second ago |
| The family story | Protagonist tells the story in their mum's exact posture — because their mum's hand is operating them from behind, like a puppet |
| Copy becomes master | Derek files the rebuild and, in the same unbroken motion, drops the original in a bin |
| Ask for the original | Close on Derek's face. Thirty-one years of experience, all of it now directed at the ceiling |
| Last frame | Protagonist opens their mouth to object. Derek's hand closes a folder over it |
