## VO

> You remember the kitchen perfectly.
>
> Yellow walls. Blue curtains. The red mug beside the sink.
>
> There were no blue curtains.
>
> You added those in twenty fourteen.
>
> And you want to tell me you're certain. I know you do. That's the part worth
> explaining.
>
> Here's what you've assumed your whole life. That remembering is opening a
> file. That the kitchen is in there somewhere, as it was, and recalling it is
> just going and fetching it.
>
> It isn't. Nobody has ever found the file. `[C1]`
>
> What you do instead is build it. Every time. Out of pieces.
>
> A researcher called Frederic Bartlett showed this in the nineteen thirties.
> He gave people an unfamiliar folk story and asked them to retell it, days
> apart. It didn't fade the way a recording fades. It got *tidier*. Strange
> details went missing. Sensible ones turned up that were never in the story. `[C2]`
>
> They weren't losing the story. They were rebuilding it — out of what they
> already knew.
>
> So let me show you where you think your memories live. And then where they
> actually live.
>
> You think it's an archive. Climate controlled, every reel labelled. You ask
> for a year, somebody fetches the reel, you watch it.
>
> What's actually back there is a workshop.
>
> They don't keep the kitchen. They keep notes about the kitchen. And when you
> ask for it, they build it — on a small set, quickly, out of whatever's on the
> shelf. Walls from one box. Light from another. Anything missing gets filled in
> from nearby.
>
> Then they wheel it out and show it to you, and it looks like the kitchen.
> Because it is made of the kitchen. Mostly.
>
> Now here's where it gets expensive.
>
> The workshop doesn't only use what it had at the time. It uses whatever is
> lying around now.
>
> In nineteen seventy-four, Elizabeth Loftus showed people a film of a car
> crash, and asked how fast the cars were going when they *hit* each other. She
> asked other people how fast they were going when they *smashed*. Same film.
> The smashed group said faster — and a week later, more of them remembered
> broken glass. `[C3][C4]`
>
> There was no broken glass. The question put it there.
>
> And you'll say you'd have noticed. There's a review from twenty twenty-four
> on exactly that — whether being sharper protects you. It helps. It genuinely
> helps. Nobody in it was immune. `[C5]`
>
> Think about a story from your childhood that gets told at every family
> gathering. Do you remember the day? Or do you remember the last time somebody
> told it? `[C6]`
>
> You can't tell from the inside. They arrive the same way.
>
> Which brings us to the part that made this famous, and the part I have to be
> careful about.
>
> In two thousand and seven, a lab tried something. Learn a list of objects on
> Monday. On Tuesday, get a small reminder of Monday — and then learn a second
> list. On Wednesday, recall the first one.
>
> The people who got the reminder put Tuesday's objects into Monday's list. Not
> the other way round. The reminder is what did it. `[C7]`
>
> There's a proposed explanation for that, and it has a name. Reconsolidation.
> The idea is that pulling a memory up leaves it briefly unsettled — open —
> before it sets again. And while it's open, what's nearby can get in. `[C11]`
>
> That's the idea. Hold on to the word *proposed*.
>
> Because if it's right, then the version they wheel out isn't just what you
> see. It's what goes back on the shelf.
>
> The copy becomes the master.
>
> And nothing back there gets flagged. Nothing is marked as repaired. The
> reconstruction goes back under the same number, in the same box, with the
> same label as the thing it replaced.
>
> You have asked for the original. Everyone has gone very quiet.
>
> Now — the honest part, and it's a real one.
>
> The behaviour is solid. Remind people, then give them something new, and the
> new thing gets into the old memory. A meta-analysis in twenty seventeen found
> that reliably. `[C8]`
>
> The mechanism is not solid. In twenty sixteen a team ran seven experiments
> trying to reproduce a well-known human demonstration of reconsolidation. They
> found nothing. Seven times. `[C13]`
>
> The reply was that the conditions weren't right. And the reply to that was:
> then tell us what the conditions are, so we can test those too. `[C14]`
>
> A major review in twenty eighteen called it a viable but hotly contested
> explanation. That is still where it sits. `[C12]`
>
> And there's a plainer story that might explain the same results. Not that the
> memory changed — that you got confused about where a detail came from. Nobody
> has ruled that out. `[C10]`
>
> While we're being honest: putting a whole false memory into someone is harder
> than people say. Not impossible. Harder. And even that is argued about. `[C15]`
>
> So what do you do with this.
>
> Not distrust yourself. That isn't available, and it isn't what the evidence
> says.
>
> Just this. Confidence isn't a measurement. The vivid ones aren't more accurate
> for being vivid. And when you and somebody else remember a room differently,
> nobody is necessarily lying. You may both be looking at a reconstruction, done
> in good faith, by a workshop that never mentions it improvises.
>
> Your memory is not useless because it changes.
>
> It changes because memory was built to keep the past useful in the present.
>
> But useful is not the same thing as exact. `[C16]`
