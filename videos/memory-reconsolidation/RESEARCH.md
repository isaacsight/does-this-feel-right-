## The finding that should shape the film

The brief asks the film to distinguish four claims. Having read the literature,
**those four claims sit at radically different evidence levels, and the gap
between them is the most interesting thing here.** The exciting claim is the
weak one.

| Tier | Claim | Evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | Memory is reconstructive, not playback | Overwhelming, century old | **Settled** |
| **2** | Post-event information can distort recall | Very strong, thousands of studies | **Settled** |
| **3** | Retrieving a memory can make it updatable | Moderate, positive meta-analysis, live alternative explanations | **Probable, mechanism unclear** |
| **4** | Reconsolidation is the biological mechanism in humans | Inconsistent; prominent failed replications | **Contested — not established** |

**This tiering is the film's spine.** Each act climbs one tier, and the honest
move is that the evidence gets *thinner* exactly as the idea gets *cooler*. The
archive metaphor survives all four tiers; the biological claim does not. That
lets the evidence section be the story's turn rather than a disclaimer — which
is what the brief asked for and what the last film had to earn the hard way.

## Tier 1 — Reconstructive memory · SETTLED

**Bartlett, F. C. (1932).** *Remembering: A Study in Experimental and Social
Psychology.* Cambridge University Press. The "War of the Ghosts" studies:
recall of an unfamiliar folk tale drifted systematically toward the
rememberer's own cultural expectations, losing unfamiliar detail and gaining
plausible substitutions. Originated the term *reconstructive*.

Not seriously disputed. Safe to state plainly and in the indicative.

## Tier 2 — Post-event misinformation · SETTLED

**Loftus, E. F. & Palmer, J. C. (1974).** "Reconstruction of automobile
destruction," *J Verbal Learning & Verbal Behavior* 13(5):585–589. Verb choice
in the question ("smashed" vs "hit") changed speed estimates and increased
false reports of broken glass a week later.

**Loftus, Miller & Burns (1978).** *J Exp Psychol: Human Learning & Memory*
4(1):19–31. The canonical post-event misinformation paradigm.

**Brassil, M., O'Mahony, C. & Greene, C. M. (2024).** "Do cognitive abilities
reduce eyewitness susceptibility to the misinformation effect? A systematic
review," *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 31(6):2410–2436. PMID 38696106.
Nine studies. Higher cognitive ability is associated with reduced
susceptibility across nearly all categories — but **"no indication that any one
trait provides total immunity to the misinformation effect."**

> *Narrower than the brief assumed* — this is a review of **moderators** of
> susceptibility, not a general review of misinformation. Cite it for "nobody
> is immune," not for the base phenomenon.

## Tier 3 — Retrieval-induced updating · PROBABLE, MECHANISM DISPUTED

**Hupbach, A., Gomez, R., Hardt, O. & Nadel, L. (2007).** "Reconsolidation of
episodic memories: a subtle reminder triggers integration of new information,"
*Learning & Memory* 14(1–2):47–53. Learn object List 1 on Day 1. On Day 2, a
subtle reminder before learning List 2. On Day 3, reminded subjects
**intermixed List 2 items into their recall of List 1** — and not the reverse.
Time-dependent. **This is the film's central experiment: it is the archive
filling a gap from a nearby box.**

**Scully, I. D., Napper, L. E. & Hupbach, A. (2017).** "Does reactivation
trigger episodic memory change? A meta-analysis," *Neurobiology of Learning and
Memory* 142(Pt A):99–107. PMID 28025069. Reactivation makes episodic memories
susceptible to interference; post-reactivation learning **reliably increased
intrusions** from new information. Effects notably stronger for older memories.

> **Read this precisely.** It supports the *behavioural phenomenon* — memories
> change after reactivation. It does **not** establish the *neurobiological
> mechanism*. The film must not let the meta-analysis launder Tier 4.

**The live alternative:** Sederberg, Gershman, Polyn & Norman (2011), *Psychon
Bull Rev* — the same results fall out of a **temporal context model** without
any destabilise-and-restabilise step. Also framed as **source confusion**
rather than memory alteration. See also "Exploring the role of context on the
existing evidence for reconsolidation of episodic memory" (PMID 30084743).

## Tier 4 — Reconsolidation as a human biological mechanism · CONTESTED

**Elsey, J. W. B., Van Ast, V. A. & Kindt, M. (2018).** "Human memory
reconsolidation: A guiding framework and critical review of the evidence,"
*Psychological Bulletin* 144(8):797–848. PMID 29792441. **The single most
important source for this film.** A wealth of observations consistent with
reconsolidation across memory types — but inconsistent findings and live
alternative explanations prevent firm neurobiological conclusions in humans.
Their phrase, which the film should honour if not quote:
**"a viable but hotly contested explanation."**

**Hardwicke, T. E., Taqi, M. & Shanks, D. R. (2016).** "Postretrieval new
learning does not reliably induce human memory updating via reconsolidation,"
*PNAS* 113(19):5206–5211. PMID 27114514. **Seven experiments** — four direct
and three conceptual replications of a prominent motor-sequence demonstration.
**No reconsolidation effects found.**

**The exchange that followed is itself the story.** Walker & Stickgold replied
that boundary conditions were not met; Hardwicke et al. replied that *"proposed
boundary conditions on memory reconsolidation will require empirical
verification."* That is the honest heart of the field: a theory that predicts
failure under unspecified conditions is hard to falsify. If the film lands one
idea from the evidence section, it should be this one.

**Further failures:** noradrenergic blockade failing to reduce conditioned fear
(PMID 25506319, 2014); fear-memory interference failure (PMID 28469565, 2017);
"Demarcating the boundary conditions of memory reconsolidation: An unsuccessful
replication" (PMID 35145138, 2022).

**The affirmative case:** Agren, T. (2014), "Human reconsolidation: a
reactivation and update," *Brain Research Bulletin* 105:70–82. PMID 24397965.
And Lee, J. L. C., Nader, K. & Schiller, D. (2017), "An Update on Memory
Reconsolidation Updating," *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 21(7):531–545.
PMID 28495311 — reviews progress *and* explicitly addresses recent challenges.
Animal foundation: Nader, Schafe & LeDoux (2000), *Nature* — verify before use.

## The counterweight the film must carry

The brief forbids implying false memories are always implanted. There is a real
literature saying implantation is **harder than popularly believed**:

**Brewin, C. R. & Andrews, B. (2017).** "Creating Memories for False
Autobiographical Events in Childhood: A Systematic Review," *Applied Cognitive
Psychology*. Some recollective experience for a suggested event is induced in
roughly **47%** of participants, but only about **15%** are rated as full
memories.

**And that is itself disputed** — Nash (2017) and McNally (2017) published
commentaries in the same journal arguing the prevalence claims are
misrepresented, and the exchange was still running in 2025 (Goodman 2025;
Brewin 2025). **Do not present 47/15 as settled numbers.** The safe formulation
is comparative: *harder than people assume, easier than nobody.*

## Claims the film must never make

Per the brief, and each one is genuinely unsupported:

- Every recalled memory becomes unstable — **no**; boundary conditions are
  precisely what is disputed.
- Remembering rewrites memory — **no**; overstates Tier 3 and assumes Tier 4.
- Reconsolidation explains human memory updating — **no**; Elsey et al.
- Vivid or traumatic memories are unreliable — **no**, and harmful.
- Confidence proves accuracy either way — **no**.
- Reconsolidation therapy reliably erases memories — **no**. Clinical work
  exists and is promising; "erase" is wrong and irresponsible.
- Lab mechanism → courtroom or clinic — **no**. Tier 2 has genuine forensic
  relevance; Tier 4 does not.
