# CLAIMS — Tipping (episode 4)

Every factual claim available to the room, with sourcing status.
Drafted 2026-08-09. Writers build ONLY on verified rows.

## Verified (web, 2026-08-09)

1. **Tipping is an aristocratic European import.** The practice traces to
   Tudor England — by the 17th century overnight guests were expected to pay
   "vails" to the host's servants. Americans imported it in the 1850s-60s,
   accelerating after the Civil War as newly rich Americans travelled to
   Europe and brought the habit home to seem aristocratic.
   - National Geographic history of tipping; Richmond Fed Econ Focus 2024;
     Wikipedia (Gratuity).

2. **America hated it first — and BANNED it.** The Anti-Tipping Society of
   America (founded 1904) claimed 100,000 members. Between 1909 and 1915,
   SEVEN states made tipping illegal — Washington, Mississippi, Arkansas,
   Iowa, South Carolina, Tennessee among them. Contemporary insults, all
   real: "a cancer in the breast of democracy," "flunkeyism," and above all
   "offensively un-American." William R. Scott's 1915 manifesto THE ITCHING
   PALM argued every tip was "a blow at our experiment in democracy."
   Enforcement was near-impossible; by 1926 every ban was repealed.
   - NPR "When Tipping Was Considered Deeply Un-American" (2015);
     Money Digest; trace histories of the seven-state bans.

3. **The joke of history: the country that banned tipping became its world
   capital** — while Europe, which invented it, largely folded service into
   wages. (State plainly; no numbers needed.)
   - Richmond Fed 2024; general histories.

4. **The serious beat, stated precisely: after the Civil War, American
   employers — hotels, restaurants, and famously the Pullman Company — used
   tipping to avoid paying wages to Black workers, many formerly enslaved.
   Pullman porters lived substantially on tips.** This is the documented
   employment-practice claim, NOT a claim that tipping "came from slavery"
   (its origin is Tudor England; the origin-framing is contested and we do
   not need it).
   - TIME (2018), History News Network, Nat Geo. PLAYED STRAIGHT — one act,
     no gags, same discipline as the war act in the pigeon episode.

5. **The wage line: the federal tipped minimum wage is $2.13/hour and has
   been frozen since 1991** — a 1996 FLSA amendment held it there, lobbied
   for by the National Restaurant Association. Inflation has roughly halved
   its real value.
   - EPI, CEPR, NELP, civilrights.org.

6. **Service quality barely explains the tip.** Michael Lynn (Cornell, the
   field's most prolific researcher): the relationship between service
   quality and tip size is "rather weak"; bill size and the fear of
   disappointing the server dominate. Lynn's documented effects on tips
   include sunshine, a server's touch on the shoulder, introducing yourself
   by name, and drawing a smiley face on the check — the tip responds to
   THEATRE more than to service.
   - Cornell News 1996; Lynn & Sturman 2010 (within-subjects nuance: some
     reliable correlation exists after controlling for tipper identity —
     the honest phrasing is "weak, not zero").

7. **The screen flip is measurable and recent** — POS tablets defaulting to
   20/25/30% with the server present; "guilt tipping" is the common term.
   (Use as observed contemporary practice, not with invented statistics.)
   - Widely reported 2023-2026; keep numeric claims OUT unless a specific
     study is added here.

## What this episode may NOT say

- NOT "TIP stands for To Insure Promptness" — the acronym etymology is
  FALSE, and stating the myth's falseness IS a usable beat.
- NOT "tipping came from slavery" — see row 4 for the precise, documented
  claim; precision is the respect the material requires.
- NOT invented percentages for screen-era tip inflation; no study, no number.
- NOT that servers prefer wage reform — workers are split; do not speak for
  them.
- The Lynn finding is "weak, not zero" — never "service doesn't matter."

## The spine (for the room)

The man just wants to buy a coffee. Between him and the door: a machine that
has turned a private kindness into a public verdict. The history explains the
trap: America imported a servility it claimed to despise, banned it, lost,
wired it into the wage law in 1991, and finally built it a monument with
three buttons. Lynn's finding is the punchline and the mercy: the jury he
fears was never grading the service. It was never about the coffee at all.
