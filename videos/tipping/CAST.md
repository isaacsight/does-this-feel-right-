# CAST & ENVIRONMENTS — Tipping (episode 4, stills catalog)

The engagement engine: tipping is a TINY PAYMENT carrying an ENORMOUS moral
weight, in public. Every character exists to make that weight visible, and
every character is defined by what they DO to somebody else — never by how
they look standing still. The standing law holds: this file is not sufficient
by itself; the board re-describes every character AT POINT OF USE with counts
of countable features.

## The cast

### THE MAN — the recurring series character
Small slight man, round face, big confident nose, EXACTLY TWO kinked hairs,
flat VERMILION RED coat, absurdly oversized shoes. His want, tiny and
absolute: TO BUY ONE COFFEE WITHOUT A MORAL EXAMINATION. He never gets it.
Interactions that define him: freezing mid-reach as the screen rotates toward
him; doing visible arithmetic with his eyes while a queue watches; holding a
single coin over a jar like a man at a wishing well with witnesses.

### THE BARISTA — the other side of the screen
A young woman, tall where he is small, apron, ONE pencil behind her ear, a
completely BLANK name badge. NEVER a villain and never pitiful — she didn't
build the screen either, and (verifiable) the tips are her actual wage. Her
signature interaction: the QUARTER-TURN — she rotates the tablet toward him
with both hands, formally, like a bailiff presenting evidence, then looks
politely into the middle distance. The comedy is that both of them are
embarrassed and neither invented the machine.

### THE SCREEN — the antagonist, and it is a machine
An oversized tablet on a counter mount, drawn like a MONUMENT: a dark slab
with THREE large blank buttons and one small apologetic button below them.
It has no face and needs none. It is always drawn slightly too big, the way
the arm-from-the-clouds was drawn in the queue episode. Where the man goes,
the slab is waiting — in the cafe, at the barbershop, in the taxi, at the
self-checkout where nobody served anybody at all.

### THE JURY — the queue behind him
FIVE people behind him in line, individuated, every one of them elaborately
NOT watching (ceilings, shoes, fingernails) with one child staring directly.
They are the queue episode's crowd grown into a courtroom function: the
audience his fear invented — and (verifiable, Lynn) the fear is the actual
mechanism, more than the service.

### THE GRAND WAITER — the history acts
A magnificent 1890s European hotel waiter: tailcoat, towering posture, tray
aloft, ONE white-gloved palm held discreetly open at hip height, eyes at the
horizon. Dignity and beggary in one body. He is how the practice ARRIVED —
aristocratic Europe, imported by travelling Americans showing off.

### THE CRUSADER — the anti-tipping leagues, played mostly straight
A 1910s reformer in a sash, pamphlets under one arm, chin up, mid-speech on
a soapbox. The astonishing verifiable fact he carries: several American
states BANNED tipping between 1909 and 1926 as un-American bribery. He lost.
The gag is history's: the country that banned tipping became its world
capital.

### THE RESEARCHER — the Lynn figure
A man in a cardigan with a clipboard, measuring everything at a diner window:
sunshine, a drawn smiley face on a check, the size of the bill — everything
EXCEPT the service. His face: mild permanent surprise at his own results.

### THE SPLIT-CHECK TABLE — the ensemble set piece
EIGHT diners at one round table after dinner, one bill in the centre like an
unexploded device, every face doing a different calculation — one with a
phone, one with a napkin of sums, one asleep, one already at the coat rack.

## Environments — the episode travels (cafe capped hard)

1. THE CAFE COUNTER — home base, capped ≤20% of frames: counter, slab, jar,
   the jury line.
2. THE GRAND HOTEL, 1890s Europe — chandeliers, marble, the Grand Waiter.
3. THE ATLANTIC LINER — the practice crossing the ocean: deck, rail, steam;
   the episode's widest landscape.
4. MAIN STREET, 1912 — the Crusader's rally: bunting, soapbox, straw hats.
5. THE STATE CAPITOL — a vast marble hall where a tiny bill is signed and,
   later, quietly unsigned.
6. THE DINER — the Researcher's window booth; sun through glass; checks and
   coffee refills.
7. THE PAYROLL OFFICE — a huge ledger, a tiny stack of coins: the $2.13 line
   (frozen since 1991 — verify before boarding).
8. THE TAXI — back seat, rear-view mirror eyes, the slab glowing in the dark.
9. THE BARBERSHOP — mirrors multiplying the jury infinitely.
10. THE NIGHT STREET — the man walking home past shuttered shops, every dark
    window holding one faint glowing slab.

## Interaction quota (feeds the board)

Every act contains at least one frame where a named character ACTS ON another
named character: the quarter-turn, the palm at hip height, the pamphlet
pressed into a hand, the bill pushed to the table's centre, the child's stare.
Lone-figure frames are rationed to the night street, where loneliness IS the
content.

## WARDROBE — fashion is the era-clock and the class argument

Wardrobe rules are consistency infrastructure: every garment below is
COUNTABLE and gets restated at point of use. Palette discipline: the series
palette (ink blue, mustard gold, sage green, dusty orange, cream, vermilion
red) is CAST-ALLOCATED — a colour belongs to a character before it belongs to
a scene.

### Principals
- THE MAN — the flat VERMILION RED coat with THREE round buttons, worn in
  every era unchanged: he is the constant the centuries happen around. Thin
  stick legs, oversized shoes. He never gets period dress — that is the joke
  of the history acts: the same man, the same coat, 1890 to now.
- THE BARISTA — cream shirt with sleeves rolled to the elbow, SAGE GREEN
  half-apron with ONE front pocket holding TWO pens, blank badge, hair in a
  high knot with the ONE pencil through it. Modern-era only.
- THE SCREEN — matte near-black slab, ONE steel counter-mount arm, THREE
  large blank buttons stacked, ONE small button beneath. Machines have
  wardrobe too: it is always clean, always newer than everything around it.
- THE JURY — five DIFFERENT palette colours, never two matching coats
  (individuation law), one child in a striped jumper (the starer).

### Period cast
- THE GRAND WAITER — black tailcoat with TWO points, white waistcoat, white
  bow tie, ONE white glove on the open palm (bare hand holds the tray), gold
  watch-chain in a shallow arc. The most formally dressed body in the film —
  formality AS the costume of servitude.
- THE CRUSADER — 1912: straw boater, rolled shirtsleeves with sleeve garters,
  MUSTARD GOLD sash lettered BLANK (type lands in post if ever), waistcoat,
  pamphlets. His crowd: boaters and cloth caps split by class, long skirts
  and shirtwaists for the women — era-correct silhouettes, simplified flat.
- THE RESEARCHER — dusty ORANGE cardigan with THREE buttons over a checked
  shirt, corduroy trousers, clipboard. The only soft-shouldered silhouette
  in the film: academia as knitwear.
- THE SPLIT-CHECK TABLE — eight silhouettes of modern dining-out dress, each
  keyed to a different palette colour, one necktie loosened, one pair of
  reading glasses pushed up a forehead.

### Era wardrobe as the clock
1890s hotel: tailcoats, gowns, gloves — vertical silhouettes, high contrast.
1912 street: boaters, caps, sashes, sleeve garters — busy, upright, buttoned.
1991 payroll office: short-sleeved dress shirt, ONE bad wide tie, high-waist
trousers (the $2.13 line deserves its era's saddest office clothes).
Now: hoodies, aprons, puffer vests — soft shapes around the hard slab.

The wardrobe test at the board: name the garment, count its features, assign
its colour — a frame that says "a waiter" instead of "a tailcoat with two
points and one gloved open palm" will drift, and the drift will be invisible
until 130 frames are paid for.
