## Angle

The film has just shown the study is thin. The viewer's next question is not
about the study, it is about themselves: *if the evidence is that light, why did
I take it so fast?* My answer is that the belief did not arrive with the
evidence. It was already in the building, and there is a paper trail.

Nicholas Carr published the worry in The Atlantic in July 2008. It is an essay.
He describes his own attention and then, in print, refuses to call that proof —
*"Anecdotes alone don't prove much"* — and asks the reader to be skeptical of
him. The experiment everybody now reaches for came three years later, and it
mentions Carr exactly once, as the last item on its reference list, inside a
sentence saying the question is *still being debated*.

So the order is: worry, then evidence. Which means the evidence failing does not
take the worry with it. That is the exit condition, and it is a date rather than
an opinion.

**The act deliberately does not name the 2011 study, the journal, or its
authors.** Naming it here would spend Act 4's opening. My act hands over a paper
with a citation in it; Act 4 says what the paper was, what it found, and how it
ended.

heard proof."* and *"We have done this before…"*.

> So how did a preliminary result become something you could repeat without
> checking it? Not from the evidence. The evidence was late. And you can put a
> date on that.
>
> In July of 2008, The Atlantic ran an essay by Nicholas Carr. You know the
> title. You have probably used the title. Is Google Making Us Stupid? Most of us
> have never read the piece underneath it. It is a man describing his own
> attention. He says reading used to be easy, and that now his concentration
> starts to drift after two or three pages. That is the same kind of evidence you
> have. And he is careful about what it is worth. Anecdotes alone don't prove
> much, he says, and we still await the long-term neurological and psychological
> experiments. Near the end he puts it plainer than that. You should be skeptical
> of my skepticism.
>
> That essay is seventeen years older than the paper you heard about last June.
> It is three years older than the experiment that came next. And when that
> experiment was published, it named Carr once. Last entry on the list of
> references. Inside a sentence saying that the disadvantages of being constantly
> wired are still being debated.
>
> So the worry was in print first, and the evidence turned up second. Which means
> you did not get this from a study. It was there before the study, and it will
> still be there when you have forgotten which study it was.

### On the reception problem the other writer flagged

The clause *"and got read as the answer"* is not in these pages and no equivalent
is. I have no named commentator saying the 2011 study settled Carr, and I did not
go looking for one, because the act does not need it. Every factual sentence here
is a date, a title, or a string in a document. The work that a reception claim
would have done is done instead by the last three sentences, which are addressed
to the viewer's own belief — a thing the viewer can check against themselves in
real time, and the only evidence in this act that needs no citation.

## Lines I would fight for

1. **"It named Carr once. Last entry on the list of references. Inside a sentence
   saying that the disadvantages of being constantly wired are still being
   debated."** The whole angle, checkable to the line, and physical — a name at
   the bottom of a list. It sets up the next act's landing word without saying it.
2. **"You should be skeptical of my skepticism."** Carr, in print, refusing the
   standing everyone later granted him. It stops the act from making him a
   villain and it is the film's own method quoted back from 2008.
3. **"It was there before the study, and it will still be there when you have
   forgotten which study it was."** The exit condition as one sentence. It is what
   makes Act 4's replication failures land as sad rather than as a scoreboard.

## Lines I am unsure of

- **"You have probably used the title."** Two `you` in six words, immediately
  after *"You know the title."* It is doing register work as much as argument
  work, and it is the first thing I would cut if the merged gate has room. If it
  goes, the act drops to 8 `you` in 242 words — 33.1 — and stops carrying itself.
  Flagging so the trade is visible rather than discovered.
- **Reported speech versus direct quotation of Carr.** I deliberately did **not**
  narrate his best sentence — *"Over the past few years I've had an uncomfortable
  sense that someone, or something, has been tinkering with my brain"* — because
  a listener with no scrollback hears *I* and *my brain* in the narrator's voice,
  and the standing rule is that the narrator is never a character. The paraphrase
  is duller and safer. But note that *"You should be skeptical of my skepticism"*
  has the same exposure, one `my` deep, and I kept it because the frame *"Near
  the end he puts it plainer than that"* arrives first and because the line is
  worth the risk. If the Director disagrees, the reported-speech version is *"Near
  the end he tells the reader to be skeptical of his skepticism"* — same fact,
  none of the snap.
- **"That is the same kind of evidence you have."** It is the mechanism of the
  whole act in eight words and it may be one beat too explicit, since the next
  paragraph shows it rather than says it. Cuttable at a cost of one `you`.

**The handoff into Act 4 needs one look.** My last paragraph ends on the belief

2011 as dates without saying what happened in 2011, and Act 4 then says it. But

**On the word I did not use.** *Footnote* appears nowhere in these pages, by
instruction, and I think the instruction is right for a second reason: the fact
is stronger unlabelled. The viewer hears *last entry on the list of references*,
and ninety seconds later hears *we went looking for a footnote*, and does the
join themselves. If my act said the word, Act 4's line would only be a repeat.

**On placement.** This act works where the brief puts it and nowhere else. Before
Act 3 it would be an aside about a 2008 essay. After Act 4 it would be the third
consecutive act about a paper from before the viewer was worried. It belongs at
the moment the viewer has just been told the study is thin and has not yet been
told this happened before — the one point in the film where *why did I believe
it?* is the live question.

**Filed and not narrated.** The sentence immediately after Carr's name in the
2011 paper reads *"It may be no more that nostalgia at this point, however, to
wish we were less dependent on our gadgets."* The typo is the paper's. It is a
gift and it is out, for the same reason the treatment kept the v1/v2 statistics
