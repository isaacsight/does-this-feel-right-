Somebody has explained you to you. Maybe it was a chemical, and you get a hit of dopamine from your phone, and that is why you keep picking it up. Maybe it was a gene, and it runs in your family, so it was always going to be you. Maybe it was a study you half remember, about what people do, which quietly meant you. You did not go looking for any of these. They were handed to you, and each one felt like it explained something, because each one showed up as a cause. A cause is a good shape. It points one way, it fits in a sentence, and it lets you stop asking. Over the last twenty years, three fields went back and checked the causes they had been giving you. A chemical, a gene, and a sample. All three made the same mistake, and we are going to take them in order, because the mistake is better than any of the three stories on its own.

Start with the chemical, because you have said this one out loud. A dopamine hit. A dopamine detox. When you use the word, you mean pleasure. You mean the good feeling. That is not what the work says, and the gap is not small, because it is closer to a flip. In 1997 Wolfram Schultz, Peter Dayan and Read Montague published a paper in Science called A Neural Substrate of Prediction and Reward. Schultz had spent years listening to single dopamine cells in monkeys, and what those cells tracked was not how nice the treat was. They tracked the gap between what was expected and what turned up. Give the animal a treat it did not see coming and they fire. Give it the treat it already knew about and they go quiet. So the signal is not pleasure. The signal is surprise. Around the same time Kent Berridge and Terry Robinson were pulling apart two things we all keep in one word. Wanting, and liking. Take dopamine away from a rat and it stops working for the food, but it still makes the face it makes when food tastes good. The wanting fell over. The liking did not. So dopamine is much closer to the reaching than to the enjoying, and when you say your phone gave you a dopamine hit, you have named the wrong half of it. The pull is the part that is dopamine. The pleasure is the part you were hoping for.

Now the gene, and this is where your first story and your second story touch. For about fifteen years there was a way to do this. You picked a gene you had some reason to suspect, you got a few hundred people, and you looked for a link with a trait. They were called candidate genes, and the most famous ones were dopamine genes. There was a receptor called D four that the papers turned into the novelty seeking gene, the gene for people who take risks and get bored fast. We should be careful here, because the honest version is duller than either headline. When Marcus Munafo and his colleagues pooled the studies, a link did survive for novelty seeking, but there was clear sign of publication bias, and it did not show up in the people at the far ends of the trait. So it is shaky and nice to publish, rather than simply wrong. The wider field got a much cleaner answer. In 2019 Richard Border and his colleagues took the eighteen genes most studied for depression, each one with more than ten papers behind it, and tested them in groups running from sixty two thousand people up to four hundred and forty three thousand. Writing in the American Journal of Psychiatry, they found those eighteen were no more linked to depression than genes picked at random. Fifteen years of the gene for something, and at that size there was nothing there.

Third, the sample, and this one is about who was in the room. In 2010 Joseph Henrich, Steven Heine and Ara Norenzayan put out a paper with a rude title. The weirdest people in the world. Weird was short for western, educated, rich, and living in a modern democracy. What they found is that about ninety six percent of the people studied in psychology came from those places, and those places hold about twelve percent of everyone alive. On its own that is only a sampling problem, and you could shrug at it. The sharp part is what happened when they lined those subjects up against everybody else. On how you see, how you share, how you reason, how you describe yourself, the western student was not the neutral middle you would assume. Again and again, they were the odd one out. The strangest group in the set was the group holding the ruler, and we are still mostly reading what they found.

Here is the fact that ties all three together, and it is one study. In 2003 Eric Turkheimer and his colleagues looked at test scores from seven year old twins, and many of those families were living near or below the poverty line. They asked how much of the difference between those children came from genes. They got two different answers. In the poorest homes, about sixty percent of it came from the home the twins shared, and the part from genes was close to nothing. In the richest homes it was almost exactly the other way round. Same trait, same test, two numbers, because two different rooms. Sit with that for a second, because it breaks something we were all told. How much a thing is inherited is not a fact about the thing. It is a fact about a group of people in the place you measured them. Move the people and the number moves. So a gene for something was never the sort of thing that could be true by itself, and a sample of twelve percent of us was never going to speak for you.

The easy ending is that none of it holds and you can drop all of it. You do not get that. The work did not vanish. Traits really are inherited, and the effect is big. Dopamine really is central, just to wanting and not to pleasure. What fell over was the single gene, the single feeling, and the single group standing in for all of us. And we owe you the obvious thing about this film, because it is the same failure. It was written and drawn and read out by machines trained on a huge pile of text that is almost all western, educated, rich and modern. Our instrument has the exact fault we have spent six minutes describing, and we cannot step outside it to check. So when the next clean answer about you shows up, and it will, the useful question is not whether it is true. It is smaller than that, and you can nearly always ask it. Who did they measure.
