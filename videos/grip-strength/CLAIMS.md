# CLAIMS — Your Grip Is a Crystal Ball (grip strength)

Every factual claim available to the script, with sourcing status.
Drafted 2026-08-09. Writers may only build on VERIFIED rows.

This is a 2-3 minute MOTION piece, so the claims list is deliberately short:
five load-bearing facts, stated precisely, is the whole budget. A short film
that hedges reads as padding; a short film that overstates gets a comment
section full of corrections. Precision is the style.

## Verified (web, 2026-08-09)

1. **The PURE study, Lancet 2015 (Leong et al.).** 142,861 participants in 17
   countries, median follow-up 4.0 years. Every 5 kg drop in grip strength was
   associated with a 16% higher risk of death from any cause (HR 1.16, 95% CI
   1.13-1.20), 17% higher cardiovascular mortality, 7% higher risk of heart
   attack, 9% higher risk of stroke. Grip strength was a STRONGER predictor of
   all-cause and cardiovascular mortality than systolic blood pressure.
   - Leong et al., "Prognostic value of grip strength," The Lancet 386(9990).

2. **UK Biobank replication, BMJ 2018 (Celis-Morales et al.).** 502,293
   participants aged 40-69. Per 5 kg lower grip: all-cause mortality HR 1.20
   (women) and 1.16 (men); cardiovascular 1.19/1.22; respiratory 1.31/1.24;
   associations across multiple cancers. Two independent cohorts totalling
   ~645,000 people say the same thing.
   - Celis-Morales et al., BMJ 2018;361:k1651.

3. **Young men's grip has measurably declined.** Fain & Weatherford (Journal of
   Hand Therapy, 2016) compared millennial norms with the 1985 norms: men aged
   20-24 averaged 121 lb of right-hand grip in 1985 and 101 lb in the study
   sample — a 20 lb drop. Men 25-29 dropped 26 lb (right hand). Women 20-24
   showed smaller but significant right-hand losses.
   - Fain & Weatherford 2016; covered by NPR, "Millennials May Be Losing
     Their Grip" (2016).

4. **The proposed mechanism for the decline is occupational, not moral.** Fain's
   own explanation: work patterns changed — less agricultural and manufacturing
   labour, more technology work. The hands stopped doing load-bearing work.
   - Fain, quoted in NPR/press coverage, 2016.

5. **Grip strength is used clinically as a proxy for overall muscle strength
   and biological age** — a dynamometer squeeze is cheap, fast, and
   risk-stratifies as well as or better than several standard vitals. That is
   why these studies keep using it: not because hands are special, but because
   the hand is an honest window on the whole body.
   - Framing supported by both papers' own rationale sections.

## What this piece may NOT say

- **NOT "weak grip kills you" / "train your grip to live longer."** Grip is a
  MARKER, not a proven cause. Both papers say so explicitly — PURE calls for
  research on "whether improvement in strength reduces mortality." Squeezing a
  gripper does not extend life; the grip reflects the body that built it. The
  script must land on marker-not-cause plainly, because that inversion is the
  intellectually honest turn AND the most interesting beat.
- **NOT "millennials are lazy."** The Fain sample is 237 volunteers from one
  region — real, published, but small. State the numbers, attribute the
  mechanism to changed work, and never moralise. The decline is an environment
  measurement, not a character assessment.
- **NOT "stronger than blood pressure" beyond what PURE measured** — that
  comparison is specifically about predictive value for mortality in that
  cohort, not "blood pressure doesn't matter."
- No "15 cigarettes"-style manufactured equivalences of any kind.

## The spine (for the room)

The dynamometer is a crystal ball that reads backwards. It cannot tell you how
to live longer; it tells you how you have been living. Two cohorts, six hundred
and forty-five thousand people: the squeeze of a hand predicts death better
than blood pressure — and the generation that stopped working with its hands
measurably lost 20 pounds of it. The turn: you cannot cheat the meter by
training the meter. The grip is the receipt, not the purchase.

## Motion notes (why this subject fits i2v)

Hands are the one subject the clip model handles best: single subject, simple
nameable motion (a hand closing, a needle sweeping, a dynamometer compressing),
no crowds, no text. The force curve and the generational bars are DATA FRAMES —
they stay still or get board-side treatments, never subject motion (playbook
