## Pages

**I.** (58) Your hand finds the phone before you're awake. // An economist,
Herbert Simon, saw this coming in 1971: looking at information costs attention,
so more information means less attention to go around. That makes it scarce.
Scarce things get priced. // Fifty-five years on, yours sells at auction in
milliseconds.

**II.** (72) You've heard the phone gives you dopamine. Close, and wrong where
it counts. // In 1997, Schultz, Dayan and Montague found what dopamine tracks:
not pleasure — surprise. The gap between what you expected and what you got. //
Get what you predicted, nothing. Get what you didn't, it spikes. // So it's not
built to make you happy. It's built to keep you guessing.

**III.** (108) The guessing has a name. In 1957, Ferster and Skinner mapped it:
reward the behaviour, but not every time, never on a pattern you can crack. //
Hardest habit they tested to break. A pigeon fed at random keeps pecking long
after a pigeon fed on schedule quits. // Now your phone. Pull to refresh — a
slot machine lever. The red badge, which says nothing but "something happened."
The feed with no bottom, which took the one thing every page had: an end. // On
purpose. In 2017 a Facebook founder said it out loud — a social-approval loop
exploiting a weakness in people. They did it anyway.

**IV.** (86) What's it cost you? Not outrage — numbers. // Leroy, 2009: get
pulled off a task before you finish, part of your head stays behind. Attention
residue. // Ward's team, 2017: memory tests with the phone on the desk, in a
pocket, in another room. Some switched it off. // Off changed nothing. Distance
did. Further away, more room in your head. // It doesn't have to buzz. Just be
within reach.

**V.** (150) That last study is the weakest thing I've shown you. // In 2022
they ran it again — analysis locked before the data. No effect. // I just spent
thirty-five seconds on that. // A year later, a review of twenty-two studies:
real, about a seventh of a standard deviation. Small. // The biggest look we
have — Orben and Przybylski, 2019 — ran every reasonable analysis at once,
across three hundred and fifty-five thousand teenagers. The link is real,
negative, at most four tenths of one percent of the story. Potatoes looked
almost as bad. Glasses, one and a half times worse. One dataset had six hundred
million defensible ways to run it. // Not me letting them off. The tricks are
real; the harm, across everyone, is small and badly measured. Both true. // A
tiny average is what a real effect looks like spread across millions it never
touched.

**VI.** (52) Festinger, 1954: you measure yourself against other people, and
with no objective yardstick you use whoever's around. // For most of history,
that was the room. // Now the room is everyone, ranked, on their best day, never
closed. // And you're in it.

**VII.** (66) Not a conspiracy. A business model, right there in the filings:
your attention, sold and resold, and what keeps it worth buying is that you
never know what's next. // You won't out-willpower it. Change the machine, not
yourself. Kill the badges. Make the rewards predictable. Boring. // Then it's
easy to put down — nothing left to wait for.
