# PAGES — ZENN VOICE, PLAINEST READ (all seven acts)

**Angle brief:** Rewrite the whole VO in the flattest, plainest version of
Zenn's voice. Short sentences. Second person. Present tense where possible. No
aphorisms, no "documentary narrator." Talk to one person across a table. A
little dry, never lofty. Keep every verified claim and citation; only the prose
changes. Word budget ~660–690.

## Lines I am unsure of
- Act I close "before you've finished blinking" — I like it but it may be too
  cute for the milliseconds claim. Fallback: "in less time than it takes to
  blink."
- Act V turn line "That last study is the weakest thing I've shown you." — the
  "I've shown you" makes the narrator a person, which is a departure from the
  old no-skin-in-the-game rule. I think Zenn *is* a person on the mic, so I
  committed to it. Flagging because it is a real change.
- Act VII ending — I killed "boring machine you can put down" per the brief and
  landed on "nothing left to wait for." It circles back to Act II. Might be too
  neat.

## Pages

**I.** You wake up. And before you're really awake, your hand already knows
where the phone is. // Back in 1971, an economist named Herbert Simon worked
something out, before any of this existed. Information isn't free to look at.
It costs you attention. So the more information there is, the less attention
you've got to spread around. // That makes attention scarce. And scarce things
get a price. // Fifty-five years later, that price gets set in an auction that
finishes before you've finished blinking.

**II.** You've heard your phone gives you little hits of dopamine. That's
close. It's also wrong in the way that matters. // In 1997, three scientists —
Schultz, Dayan and Montague — worked out what dopamine actually tracks. Not
pleasure. Surprise. The gap between what you expected and what you got. // Get
exactly what you predicted, and not much happens. Get something you didn't see
coming, and it spikes. // So the machine isn't built to make you happy. It's
built to keep you guessing.

**III.** That guessing has a name. In 1957, two researchers, Ferster and
Skinner, mapped what happens when a reward comes — but not every time, and never
on a pattern you can crack. // Of everything they tested, it was the hardest
habit to break. A pigeon fed at random keeps pecking long after a pigeon fed
like clockwork gives up. // Now look at your phone. Pull to refresh: that's the
slot machine lever. The little red badge, which tells you nothing except that
something, somewhere, happened. And the feed with no bottom, which took away the
one thing every page used to give you. A place to stop. // And it was on
purpose. In 2017 one of Facebook's founders said it out loud: they'd built a
social-approval loop that exploits a weakness in human psychology. And — his
words — they did it anyway.

**IV.** So what does it actually cost you? Not in outrage. In numbers. //
Sophie Leroy, 2009: start something, get pulled away before you finish, and
part of your head stays stuck on the first thing. She called it attention
residue. // Adrian Ward and his team, 2017: people took memory tests with the
phone on the desk, in a pocket, or in another room. Some were told to switch it
all the way off. // Switching it off did nothing. Distance did. The further away
the phone, the more they could hold in their head. // The phone doesn't have to
buzz. It just has to be close enough to grab.

**V.** Okay. That last study is the weakest thing I've shown you. // In 2022,
someone ran it again — properly, with the whole analysis locked in before they
looked at the data. Nothing. No effect. // I just spent thirty-five seconds on
that. // A year later, a review pooled twenty-two of these studies. The effect's
real — and it's about a seventh of a standard deviation. Small. // Then the
biggest look we have: Amy Orben and Andrew Przybylski, 2019. Every reasonable
version of the analysis at once, across three hundred and fifty-five thousand
teenagers. The link between screen time and feeling bad is real, and negative,
and at most four tenths of one percent of what's going on. In the same data,
eating potatoes looked almost as bad for you. Wearing glasses looked one and a
half times worse. And in one of those datasets alone, there were six hundred
million defensible ways to run the numbers. // So, to be clear: this isn't me
letting them off. The tricks are real. The harm, spread across everyone, is
small and badly measured. Both are true at once. // A tiny average is just what
a real effect looks like when you spread it across millions of people it never
touched.

**VI.** Leon Festinger, 1954: you work out how you're doing by looking at other
people. When there's no objective yardstick, you use whoever's around. // For
most of history, whoever's around meant the people in the room. A few dozen
faces. // Now the room is everyone. Ranked. On their best day. And it never
closes. // And you're in it.

**VII.** None of this is a conspiracy. It's a business model, and it's sitting
right there in the filings. They sell your attention, over and over, and what
keeps it worth selling is that you never quite know what you'll get next. //
You're not going to out-willpower that. Nobody does. So change the machine
instead of yourself. Turn off the badges. Make the rewards predictable. Make
them boring. // And then it gets easy to put down — because there's nothing left
to wait for.
