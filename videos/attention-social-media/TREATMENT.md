## Thesis

*(Unchanged.)* The machinery that harvests your attention is real, old, and
documented — and the honest measurement of its population-level harm is far
smaller than the discourse admits; the film ships both facts together because a
ledger that only reports one side is an advertisement.

## The turn

**On the first line of Act V: "Okay. That last study is the weakest thing I've
shown you."**

*(Function unchanged from prior revisions; only the wording is now plain.)*
Until that moment the viewer has been building a case: the arithmetic, the
mechanism, the schedule, the confession, the phone-on-the-desk cost. The turn
is the film taking its own best evidence apart — the phone study from thirty-odd
seconds earlier fails a pre-registered replication, and then the largest
analysis in the field puts the whole effect somewhere near eating potatoes.

What changes in the viewer's model: they came in expecting to hear the harm is
enormous, and they leave knowing the *mechanism* is precise while the
*magnitude* is small. A genuinely different belief, not a louder version of the
one they arrived with.

**Timecode is provisional.** On the projected table the turn sits at ~2:22
(≈54% of runtime). Sound sets the real number after the re-record. The turn is
still Act V's *first* sentence, so the act break and the turn are the same
marker.

## Locked VO — the Zenn-voice rework

Read plain and conversational, ~160 wpm. Sentence breaks are real breaths; act
breaks are full stops with picture running. The narrator is a person talking to
one person — allowed to say "I" and to level with the viewer.

### ACT I — THE ARITHMETIC

> Watch your hand for a second. You wake up, and before your eyes are even
> open, it already knows where the phone is.
>
> An economist named Herbert Simon saw this coming in 1971. Looking at
> information costs you attention. So the more information there is, the less
> attention you've got to go around. That makes it scarce. And scarce things
> get a price.
>
> Fifty-five years later, somebody's buying yours — and the sale closes in
> milliseconds.

### ACT II — WHAT DOPAMINE ACTUALLY DOES

> You've heard the phone gives you little hits of dopamine. That's close. It's
> also wrong in the way that counts.
>
> In 1997, three scientists — Schultz, Dayan and Montague — worked out what
> dopamine actually tracks. Not pleasure. Surprise. The gap between what you
> expected and what you got.
>
> Get exactly what you predicted, and not much happens. Get something you
> didn't see coming, and it spikes.
>
> So the machine isn't built to make you happy. It's built to keep you
> guessing.

### ACT III — THE PLAYBOOK

> That guessing has a name. In 1957, two researchers, Ferster and Skinner,
> mapped what happens when a reward comes — not every time, and never on a
> pattern you can crack.
>
> Of everything they tested, it was the hardest habit to break. A pigeon fed at
> random keeps pecking long after a pigeon fed like clockwork gives up.
>
> Now look at your own phone. Pull to refresh — that's the slot machine lever.
> The red badge, which tells you nothing except that something, somewhere,
> happened. And the feed with no bottom, which took the one thing every page
> used to have. An end.
>
> And it was on purpose. In 2017, one of Facebook's founders said it out loud:
> they'd built a social-approval loop that exploits a weakness in human
> psychology. And — his words — they did it anyway.

### ACT IV — THE COST, MEASURED

> So what does it actually cost you? Not in outrage. In numbers.
>
> Sophie Leroy, 2009: start something, get pulled away before you finish, and
> part of your head stays behind. She called it attention residue.
>
> Adrian Ward's team, 2017: people took memory tests with the phone on the
> desk, in a pocket, or in another room. Some switched it all the way off.
>
> Switching it off did nothing. Distance did. The further away the phone, the
> more they could hold.
>
> It doesn't have to buzz. It just has to be within reach.

### ACT V — THE HONEST NUMBER · **THE TURN**

> Okay. That last study is the weakest thing I've shown you.
>
> In 2022, someone ran it again — properly, with the whole analysis locked in
> before they looked at the data. Nothing. No effect.
>
> I just spent thirty-five seconds on that.
>
> A year later, a review pooled twenty-two of these studies. The effect's real
> — and it's about a seventh of a standard deviation. Small.
>
> Then the biggest look we have: Amy Orben and Andrew Przybylski, 2019. Every
> reasonable version of the analysis at once, across three hundred and
> fifty-five thousand teenagers. The link between screen time and feeling bad is
> real, and negative, and at most four tenths of one percent of what's going on.
> In the same data, eating potatoes looked almost as bad for you. Wearing
> glasses looked one and a half times worse. And in one of those datasets alone,
> there were six hundred million defensible ways to run the numbers.
>
> So, to be clear: this isn't me letting them off. The tricks are real. The
> harm, spread across everyone, is small and badly measured. Both are true at
> once.
>
> A tiny average is just what a real effect looks like when you spread it across
> millions of people it never touched.

### ACT VI — THE TRIBE AT SCALE

> Leon Festinger, 1954: you work out how you're doing by looking at other
> people. When there's no objective yardstick, you use whoever's around.
>
> For most of history, whoever's around meant the people in the room. A few
> dozen faces.
>
> Now the room is everyone. Ranked. On their best day. And it never closes.
>
> And you're in it.

### ACT VII — THE CLOSE

> None of this is a conspiracy. It's a business model, and it's right there in
> the filings. They sell your attention, over and over, and what keeps it worth
> selling is that you never quite know what you'll get next.
>
> You're not going to out-willpower that. Nobody does. So change the machine
> instead of yourself. Turn off the badges. Make the rewards predictable and
> boring.
>
> And then it gets easy to put down — because there's nothing left to wait for.

- If Act IV lands at 33–37s, keep "thirty-five."
- If it lands longer, change the spoken number to the nearest value that

whose whole job is honest measurement. The specific true number performs the
thesis; a round guess undercuts it. Director confirms the final number against

## Register

**Narrator voice.** *(Rewritten for the new voice. Narrator is still Eric,
re-recorded once this locks.)* Plain, direct, second-person. A curious friend
who has read the studies and is telling you what's in them across a table — not
a broadcaster, not an essayist. Present tense where it fits. Dry, occasionally
funny, never arch, never lofty, never sloganeering. He is allowed to say "I" and
to level with you ("this isn't me letting them off"), because the whole appeal
of the voice is that a real person is talking. Authority comes from being
specific and unbothered, never from volume or gravitas.

**Emotional temperature.** Relaxed and conversational, ~5/10, warm but
unsentimental. The film gets *quieter and flatter* exactly where a lesser film
would get louder — the turn especially.

**What this film must never feel like.** An alarm. A TED talk. A LinkedIn post.
A documentary voice-of-God. A guru. If a line sounds like it's reaching for
profundity, it's wrong. If it sounds like it wants something from the viewer,
it's wrong. The argument is that calm attention is possible; a film that
performs concern has refuted itself in the delivery.

**Direction for Sound on the turn.** **"Okay. That last study is the weakest
thing I've shown you"** must be read *flatter* than everything around it — no
lift, no emphasis on "weakest," almost a shrug. A person admitting a thing
they've already made peace with. Likewise **"I just spent thirty-five seconds on
that"** is a dry aside, never rueful; and the closing **"A tiny average is just
what a real effect looks like when you spread it across millions of people it
never touched"** is the quietest, slowest line in the film — a fact being set
down, not a point being scored.

## Intent notes — per act

Feelings, not frames. The Art Director owns every image decision, including how
the character is drawn (crude, charming line work; no realistic faces per house
style — read the figure from posture, hands, and gesture).

**Act I — recognition, then a small chill.** The wake-up-and-reach is something
the viewer has done this morning. Play it plainly, almost funny, then let the
"someone is buying yours" land quietly. Space and calm; the film isn't arguing
yet.

**Act II — the pleasure of being corrected.** This should feel *good* — the
viewer swaps a wrong model for a better one and gets to enjoy the upgrade. The
tap-tap-nothing-then-spike is a small comic rhythm; let it be a little funny
before it's a little unsettling.

**Act III — recognition sharpening into discomfort.** The viewer inventories
their own phone before the narrator finishes the list. Describe a machine, never
indict the user. The founder beat lands as quiet confirmation — the thing you
suspected, said by someone who'd know — not a gotcha.

**Act IV — quiet subtraction.** The coolest air in the film. Something leaking
away with no event to point at. The reach-and-stop is the emotional centre:
wanting, unresolved, undramatic. No menace; the phone is just *there*.

**Act V — relief, and the film earning its keep.** The temperature rises here in
trust, not volume. This is the moment the viewer realises they're being levelled
with. The pull-back to the crowd should feel like opening a window, not a
downer. The boundary line gets silence on both sides — it's the door Act VI
walks through.

**Act VI — the one that stings.** After the relief, one quiet point that never
gets qualified. Intimacy first (the small circle), then a scale the body was
never built to follow. Vertigo arriving politely. The picture must **not** let
the eye count the wall — the film just spent an act insisting on honest
magnitudes; an image that invites a headcount hands the argument back. Loss of
edge, not multiplication.

**Act VII — resolution and a returned hand.** The warmth of Act I, now earned.
The viewer should feel *capable*, not scolded. The setting-change and the phone
going face-down is a small, doable gesture, not a triumph. Evening light, a day
closing properly. "Nothing left to wait for" is a door closing gently, not a
mic drop.
