# TIMING SPEC — the cut rhythm

> Written 2026-07-24 after Isaac's note: *"we got to work on the timing of the
> cuts as well."* This governs the stills cut. With no motion and no
> transitions, **cut rhythm is the only expressive tool the film has.**

## The problem being fixed

The first stills cut ran **30 images across 271.91s — a 9.1s average hold.**
Zenn and comparable explainers change the picture every 2–4 seconds. We were
roughly three times too slow, which reads as a slideshow regardless of how
good the individual frames are.

**Target: ~75 images, ~3.6s average.** But the average is a by-product, never
the method. Never divide the runtime by the frame count.

## Comic timing is UNEVEN timing

Uniform 3-second cutting would fix the drag and still kill every joke. The
rhythm has to breathe:

| Beat | Hold | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Quick run** | 1.5–2.5 s | List-y or escalating passages. Rapid images build momentum and make a gag land harder when it arrives. |
| **Setup** | 3–5 s | Let the viewer read the situation before the punchline. A gag with no setup hold is a gag nobody sees. |
| **Snap (punchline)** | 1.0–2.0 s | Hard, fast cut on the funny beat. The snap IS the joke. Proven already: the 1.7 s cut on "And you're in it." |
| **Breath** | 5–8 s | After the biggest beat, and through the turn. Earned stillness, not drift. |

## Rules

1. **Cut on the sentence, never mid-phrase.** The image lands ~8 frames before
   the words it serves, so the viewer reads the picture then hears the line
   land on it.
2. **Punchlines get the snap.** Find every comic beat in the VO and cut hard on
   it. The image should arrive with the joke, not trail it.
3. **Escalating passages accelerate.** Where the narration lists or piles up
   (the playbook in Act III, the mechanisms), shorten holds progressively so
   the cutting itself feels like mounting pressure.
4. **The turn breathes.** Act V is the honest beat — the film's most spacious
   act. Slow the cutting there deliberately, so the contrast reads as gravity
   rather than sag.
5. **No hold over ~8 s, ever.** With zero motion, a static image past 8s dies.
   If a slot needs longer, cut back to a matched earlier image or add a frame.
6. **Vary neighbours.** Never three consecutive holds of the same length. Sameness
   is what makes cutting invisible; variation is what makes it feel authored.
7. **Cut-backs are free and useful.** Returning to a thematically-matched earlier
   image reads as a callback and buys a cut where no new frame exists — but it
   fails if reused too often or without thematic reason.

## Act shape

Pace tracks the argument, not the clock:

| Act | Feel | Cutting |
|---|---|---|
| I — the arithmetic | Opening breath | Moderate; one long hold to establish, then tighten |
| II — dopamine | The first real gag territory | Setup/snap pairs |
| III — the playbook | Fastest act in the film | Quick runs, escalating |
| IV — the cost | Heavier, more deliberate | Mid holds, fewer snaps |
| V — the turn | Most spacious | Long breaths; the film slows to be believed |
| VI — the tribe | Tightening again | Quick, building to the snap |
| VII — the close | Settling, warm | Slowing to a resolved final hold |

## Non-negotiables carried from Isaac's direction

- **Still images only.** No motion of any kind — no camera moves, no draw-on,
  no keyframed scale/position/crop.
- **No transitions.** No dissolves, no fades. Hard cut to the next image, always.
- **The craft is timing.** That is the entire remaining job.
