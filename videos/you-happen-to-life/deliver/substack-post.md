There is a line you have heard before. Maybe not in these exact words, but you will know the shape of it right away.

I happen to life. Life doesn't happen to me.

You know the room it comes from. Somebody says it on a stage, or on a podcast, or in a video that arrived without you ever asking for it. And there is something in it that works. It sounds brave. It sounds like the thing a person says in the last few seconds before they finally change.

You have probably said a version of it yourself. So have I. Which is why this is worth seven minutes and why it is not a joke at anybody's expense.

Because that line is not only a mood. It points at something real, with a name and a scale and sixty years of study behind it.

## It is a real thing

In 1966 a psychologist named Julian Rotter built a test that sorts people along a single line.

At one end you believe that what happens to you comes mostly out of what you do - your work, your choices, your effort. At the other end you believe it comes mostly out of luck, or timing, or people who hold more power than you do. He called it locus of control.

The internal end looks good. People who score there tend to do better in school, keep steadier health, cope better with hard news, and quit less often when a thing turns difficult.

But hold on to one detail, because everything turns on it. **The test does not measure how much control you have. It measures how much control you feel.**

## The numbers

The same room makes a second claim, usually in the same breath: the difference between the people who make it and the people who don't is dedication.

That one is testable, and in 2014 three researchers tested it. They pulled together every study they could find on practice and skill, across music, games, sport, school, and ordinary paid work, and asked a plain question. Out of the whole gap between the good and the great, how much does practice actually explain?

| Domain | Variance explained |
|---|---|
| Games | 26% |
| Music | 21% |
| Sports | 18% |
| Education | 4% |
| Professions | **under 1%** |

Read that last one again and hold it against your own working week. Practice explains the most about chess and almost nothing about the thing you actually do for a living.

That is not a claim that practice is useless - nobody has ever got good at anything without it. It is a claim about the gap between you and the next person. Practice is the price of the ticket. It turns out not to be the reason one person ends up further along than the next.

## Which way does it point

Now the part that turns the whole thing around.

Feeling in control tracks with being in control. People with steady money and safe work and some say over their own day tend to score internal. People whose hours change without warning, whose rent moves under them, who get told rather than asked, tend to score external.

Which raises a question you cannot un-ask once you have heard it. When somebody tells you that they happen to life, are you hearing a cause, or are you hearing a report?

Some of it really is a cause. Belief does real work and we should not pretend otherwise. But some of it is only an honest reading of a life that has slack in it. They may not be steering any harder than you are. They may simply have been given more room in which to steer.

## Where it turns cruel

And here is where it stops being interesting and starts being a problem.

The same dial that predicts how much authorship you feel over your own life predicts something else entirely: how you explain a stranger.

People who score internal are more likely to explain poverty by pointing at the poor - at bad choices and weak effort. People who score external are more likely to point at the shape of the thing everybody is standing in.

It is one dial, and we all have one fitted. Wherever you have it set for yourself is roughly wherever you have it set for everybody else.

So when that line lands on the right person at the right moment, it lifts them. And when it lands on somebody who has no room at all, it turns into a diagnosis wearing the coat of encouragement. You are not stuck, you are simply not trying hard enough. Said kindly. Said often. Said, most of the time, by people who were never tested that way.

We should be slow here. That sentence is an easy one to say, and we have all been on the wrong side of it in both directions.

## What survives

Not nothing. The belief still works inside the range it can genuinely reach, and inside the room you already have, acting like the author of your day beats acting like a passenger in it by a wide margin. That part is worth keeping.

What does not survive is the size of the claim. It is not a law of the world, and it is not the gap between the people who make it and the people who don't. It is one small engine doing honest work inside conditions we did not pick.

And it arrives with a duty attached. If you have got the feeling, some of it was handed to you rather than earned by you, and the respectable thing to do with a gift like that is not to sell it back at a markup to the people who were handed less of it.

You can happen to life. You just cannot take full credit for the life you happened to.
