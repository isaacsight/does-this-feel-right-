## VO — draft, not locked

> Your heart is going. You're standing outside a door, about to meet someone,
> and your heart is going.
>
> You know what that is. That's nerves.
>
> And you don't know that. You guessed. You guessed so fast it didn't feel
> like guessing.
>
> Here's what happened, in order. Your body moved first. The explanation
> arrived second. You never see the gap, so you assume there isn't one.
>
> Let me show you the same heart three times.
>
> Here's the first. A date. Heart at a hundred and ten. You call it
> attraction.
>
> Here's the second. A car park, late, footsteps somewhere behind you. Heart
> at a hundred and ten. You call it fear.
>
> And here's the third. The start line of a race. Heart at a hundred and ten.
> You call it ready.
>
> Same body. Same number. Three completely different feelings.
>
> So who's choosing which one it is?
>
> You'll say you are. You'll say you can tell the difference.
>
> Something in you takes the signal from your body, looks around the room, and
> makes the call. The body says how much. The room says what of.
>
> The sensing part has a name. Interoception. Your body reporting on itself —
> heart, gut, lungs, temperature — underneath everything, all day, whether you
> asked or not.
>
> And here's the part nobody enjoys. That reporting is not very good.
>
> Sit still and try to count your own heartbeats without touching yourself.
> Most people are bad at it. Quite a lot of people are simply guessing from
> what they think their heart ought to be doing.
>
> So the evidence is thin. And the verdict is instant, and total, and it
> arrives with no doubt in it at all.
>
> You've never once thought: my heart is at a hundred and ten, cause unknown.
> You get a finished feeling. You get it before you get anything else.
>
> And you'll want to argue with that. Your hands are shaking.
>
> Now — the honest part.
>
> The famous experiment here is from nineteen sixty-two. Give people adrenaline,
> put them in a room with someone cheerful, and they report feeling cheerful.
> Someone angry, and they report anger. Same drug. Different room.
>
> It is a beautiful result and it has not replicated well. People have tried.
> The picture that came out is messier than the one that went in.
>
> What has held up is smaller and more useful. When researchers look for a
> clean bodily signature for each emotion — a heart rate that means fear, a
> heart rate that means joy — they don't find one. The body is real. It just
> doesn't say which.
>
> So the strong version is wrong. Your body does not invent your emotions and
> hand you a story afterwards. Context does this, and so does memory, and
> learning, and what you were taught to call things, and half a dozen other
> signals arriving at once.
>
> But the weak version holds, and the weak version is the one that helps.
>
> Your first explanation is an explanation. It is not a reading.
>
> The signal is real. It's the caption underneath it that's a draft.
>
> You can notice the heart, and doubt the sentence, and still get on the train.

## Two deliberate departures from the brief

**The advice is smaller than "relabel your anxiety as excitement."** That
reframing result exists but rests on a small literature and should not be the
payoff of a film that just spent a minute on replication failure. The ending
offers doubt, not a technique — which is also the stronger line.

**The character never wins or concedes.** In the last film the hero was
dismantled and stayed dismantled. Here the final image gives the detective a
second folder — the character doesn't get smarter, the office does, slightly.
