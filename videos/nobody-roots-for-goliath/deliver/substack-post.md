# They Booed the Best Player Alive

*Why nobody roots for Goliath - and what our sympathy for the underdog actually costs us.*

[YOUTUBE EMBED: https://youtu.be/NCXlqwE3QIU]

He did everything right. He trained, he turned up, he never cheated, and he won. And his own crowd boos him. All he wants - for the entire film - is for somebody to say well done, and he is not going to get it.

**It is not a bad night, it is a finding.** In 2007 Vandello, Goldschmied and Richards ran it properly: more rooting, more sympathy, more identification with whoever was struggling. And not only in sport - they ran it on business competitions and artistic ones, same result every time. They also checked something useful. They asked whether we want the underdog to win or whether we just want the favourite to lose, and the wanting-him-to-win was stronger. So the crowd is not out to get him. They have simply forgotten he is there, which is worse.

**Why the lean happens.** When you look at two people and one has less power, you start borrowing that person's feelings - not their argument, their feelings. And then you decide what each of them is feeling, and you decide two completely different things. The big one, you assume, feels contempt, pride, the flat satisfaction of a man who expected this. The small one feels gratitude and embarrassment and a slight apology for being here.

Our man is not feeling any of that. He is mostly worried about his knee.

**The trap.** He cannot mention it. The moment he says anything - even mildly, even reasonably - it reads as exactly the contempt they already assigned him. The complaint is the evidence. There is no sentence available to him.

**So he tries harder, and it makes it worse.** Kim and colleagues, 2008, and the title of the paper does the work: "Rooting for (and Then Abandoning) the Underdog." People back the underdog and drop him the moment he stops being one. The affection was never a verdict about him. It was a verdict about a position. He changed position, and it left with the position.

Think about the arrangement he was offered. Try hard, come from nothing, get good. He did all three, on time and in full - and completing the deal is the thing that voided it. The affection had a condition attached, and the condition was that he keep losing.

**And now the part that is about us.** Ask what our support of the little guy actually cost. Nothing. It was a feeling in a seat - no money on it, no stake, and generosity is remarkably easy when it is free. Put something on the result and the sympathy quietly reorganises itself, because attaching yourself to a winner is how you get some standing back. Our loyalty to the underdog turns out to be a luxury good. It is the thing we buy when we can afford it.

That is the honest version of what we have been calling decency. It is not fake. It is just cheaper than we thought.

Once you know the shape you will find it everywhere and none of it will be about sport. The colleague you championed right up until they got promoted. The band you loved until other people loved them. The little restaurant you told everybody about, and then it opened a second one and something in you went quiet. Every time, the story we tell ourselves is that they changed. Every time, what changed was their position.

**What to do about it**, and it is not to stop liking underdogs. Notice the flip - when your warmth toward somebody drops for no reason you can name and it happens to be the same month they succeeded, you have caught it live. Then separate two things we keep stuck together: do you like this person, or do you like their position? And here is the test, which takes four seconds. Whatever contest you are watching, ask what you would want to happen if you had money on it. If the answer changes, then whatever you were feeling was never a judgment about who deserves it.

Because the crowd is never wrong about who to root for. The crowd is just telling you what it costs them.
