# CAST — The Lonely Chapter

The recurring world's cast book. Counts are LAW and appear at point of use in
every frame that stages the character (a reference sheet is not sufficient).

## Principals

**THE MAN** — the catalog's recurring protagonist, same design in every
century: a small slight man with a round face and a big confident nose, his
head bald and smooth except for a single lonely PAIR of hairs at the very top
of his crown — one thin hair kinking left, one leaning right — a flat
VERMILION RED coat with THREE round buttons, thin stick legs, ABSURDLY
oversized shoes. WANT: to call one friend he already has. THE WARM-MAN LAW:
his coat and skin stay warm in any blue dusk.

**THE FRIEND** — a big warm man in a SAGE GREEN jacket over a mustard
waistcoat, ONE flat cap, a broad open face built for beaming, heavier and a
head taller than the Man. WANT: the identical call, equally unable. He is
never staged as an object of pity; his living room mirrors the Man's.

**THE TELEPHONE** — the episode's monument object: a matte near-black
smartphone lying FACE UP on a coffee table, its dark screen showing nothing;
when it finally lights, it shows ONE glowing blank rounded rectangle. Drawn
slightly too large, the way the tipping slab was — an object with gravity.

## The 1924 company (PROCESSION accent frames)

**THE GRAND MASTER** — a large beaming man in a dark suit with ONE mustard
GOLD ceremonial collar, ONE gavel, a watch-chain in a shallow arc.
**THE LODGE RANKS** — TWELVE men in dark suits with mustard ceremonial
collars and completely BLANK sashes, individuated faces and builds, arranged
in TWO facing rows. Banners overhead: BLANK cloth. Candles lit.
**THE GREAT-GRANDFATHER** — the Man himself, unchanged red coat, among the
ranks (the same one-design-every-century trick as tipping).

## Supporting

**THE PORTRAIT SITTERS** — two 19th-century men in dark suits and beards,
holding hands / arm around shoulder in a photographer's studio, faces easy
and unremarkable; the photographer under ONE dark cloth behind ONE bellows
camera on a tripod with THREE wooden legs.
**THE LEAGUE** — five bowlers in matching sage shirts (BLANK backs), ONE
ball rack, ONE lane lit among dark lanes for the modern frames.
**THE RESEARCHER** — a woman in an ink-blue cardigan with THREE buttons, ONE
clipboard, measuring; appears in the straight act and the Hall act only.
**THE PARTY NEXT DOOR** — warm window crowds: silhouetted groups in lit
mustard interiors, always at least THREE figures, always mid-laugh, never
individuated (they are light, not people).

## Environments (10 — living rooms capped at ≤22% of frames combined)

1. the Man's living room, dusk (EMBER)
2. the Friend's living room, dusk (the mirror — staged to rhyme, different
   wallpaper: sage vs dusty orange)
3. the sidewalk outside a small restaurant, midday (the backward goodbye)
4. the lodge hall, 1924 (PROCESSION — full pageantry)
5. the same lodge hall today: event-venue emptiness, stacked chairs (the ONE
   mirrored-empty frame)
6. the photographer's studio, 1870s (portrait act)
7. the bowling alley: league era warm and full / modern one-lane-lit
8. the back porch with two chairs and a cooler (VACANCY staging)
9. the study of files (Harvard act: ONE long shelf of matching folders,
   ONE window, dust in the light — straight act gravity)
10. the night street of lit windows (EMBER connective tissue — the man
    walking home past other people's warmth)

## Wardrobe / palette allocation

Vermilion is the Man's alone. Sage = the Friend + the league. Mustard = the
lodge (collars, light) and every warm window. Ink blue = dusk fields + the
Researcher. Dusty orange = the Friend's living room + modern interiors.
Era-clock: 1870s (frock coats, beards), 1924 (suits, collars, watch-chains),
late-seventies (bowling shirts), today (the Man's unchanged coat — he wears
the same red coat in every century).

## Interaction quota

Two-sided frames wherever the script pairs the silence: the
two living rooms are staged as mirrors at least THREE times; the goodbye
stages both men; the porch stages the empty chair as a character. No frame
of the Man alone in flat despair — his loneliness is always drawn as
geometry (thresholds, panes, vacant seconds), never as a slumped figure in
a void.
