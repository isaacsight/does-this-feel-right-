## Why this script is the model

This is the comedic voice the previous episode kept missing. The jokes come from
**specific absurd images** — "Justice demands lumbar support", "tactical
pajamas", "a wizard fighting a refrigerator", "an exhausted accountant wearing
swimming goggles" — not from wry observation. Every laugh is a *picture*, which
means every laugh is already a frame brief.

It also does the honesty section the right way: the limitations are the funniest
scene in the film ("Release the penguin costume"), not a disclaimer bolted on.

## VO — as written

[COLD OPEN]

> Scientists recently discovered a possible way to make strangers kinder.
> Was it therapy? Meditation? A revolutionary education program?
> No. They put Batman on a subway.
> Because sometimes science begins with a serious question — and sometimes
> someone at a university says: "Give Francesco the cape. We're going to Milan."

[TITLE] **Can Batman Make You a Better Person?**

[SCENE 1 — THE EXPERIMENT]

> Researchers conducted a behavioral experiment on the Milan metro.
> A woman wearing a prosthetic pregnancy belly entered a crowded train.
> Researchers then recorded whether anyone offered her a seat.
> In the control condition, she entered normally.
> In the experimental condition, another researcher entered through a different
> door dressed as Batman.
> To be clear, Batman didn't help. He didn't speak. He didn't dramatically point
> at a passenger and growl: "Justice demands lumbar support."
> He just stood about three meters away, being Batman on public transportation.
> This is an important scientific technique known as "making the morning commute
> extremely confusing."

[SCENE 2 — THE RESULTS]

> Without Batman, passengers offered the apparently pregnant woman a seat about
> 38% of the time. With Batman present, that number rose to roughly 67%.
> That means the odds of someone helping were more than three times higher in
> the Batman condition.
> Apparently, Gotham could have avoided years of violent crime if Bruce Wayne
> had simply ridden public transportation near minor ethical decisions.
> A criminal approaches a convenience store. Batman appears. The criminal
> pauses. "You know what? I'm going to hold the door for this gentleman."
> Crime solved.

[SCENE 3 — THE WEIRDEST PART]

> But the strangest result came afterward. Researchers questioned the passengers
> who surrendered their seats. Of those who helped while Batman was present,
> approximately 44% said they hadn't even noticed him.
> Excuse me?
> A grown man entered a train wearing a cape, pointed cowl, and giant bat logo —
> and almost half the helpful passengers said: "Batman? No, didn't see him."
> What normally happens on the Milan metro? A wizard fighting a refrigerator?
> Three Spider-Men arguing over a ticket?
> "Batman wasn't unusual. But did you see that tourist standing on the left side
> of the escalator? Terrifying."

[SCENE 4 — THE BEHAVIORAL SCIENCE]

> The researchers weren't claiming Batman possesses a kindness beam.
> Their idea was that unexpected events can interrupt our automatic behavior.
> Most of daily life runs on mental autopilot. Wake up. Check phone. Find
> coffee. Commute. Avoid eye contact. Pretend you didn't hear someone playing a
> video without headphones.
> Autopilot is useful. Without it, every morning would require forty-five
> minutes of conscious decision-making. "Should socks go before shoes? We need a
> committee."
> But autopilot also narrows our attention. We become focused on our
> destination, our phone, or the private argument we're winning inside our head.
> Then something unexpected happens. Batman boards the train.
> Your brain wakes up: "This is new. Scan the environment. Is there danger? Is
> this promotional? Why is his cape touching that man's newspaper?"
> That interruption may make you notice other details — including someone who
> needs your seat.
> The researchers connected this to present-moment awareness: briefly snapping
> out of routine may make us more responsive to people around us.
> Batman may not be producing kindness directly. He may simply be pressing the
> brain's refresh button.

[SCENE 5 — BUT DID BATMAN REALLY CAUSE IT?]

> Before every city purchases a municipal Batman, there are limitations.
> This was a quasi-experimental field study involving 138 metro rides in one city.
> It also compared "Batman" with "no Batman." It did not compare Batman with:
> a pirate; a giant banana; an exhausted accountant wearing swimming goggles;
> Superman, who would probably make the train uncomfortable by hovering.
> So researchers can't yet tell whether the effect came from surprise — or
> specifically from seeing a famous hero associated with helping people.
> Batman might have reminded passengers to behave heroically. Or the costume
> might have disrupted the normal atmosphere. Or passengers thought: "That
> masked man is judging us, and he appears financially unstable enough to act
> on it."
> The researchers themselves say more experiments are needed before we
> understand the mechanism.
> That is scientific language for: "Release the penguin costume."

[SCENE 6 — WHAT THIS TEACHES US]

> The larger lesson isn't that everyone should dress like Batman. Although
> public transportation would become significantly more theatrical.
> The lesson is that our surroundings influence our behavior, often without our
> conscious awareness.
> We like to imagine morality works like this: "I studied the situation,
> reviewed my ethical principles, and chose compassion."
> But sometimes it works like this: "I saw a cape, my brain rebooted, and now an
> elderly gentleman has my seat."
> Small disruptions — public art, unusual signs, playful performances, or
> changes in routine — might interrupt our tunnel vision and help us notice one
> another.
> The important word is might. One clever field study is evidence worth
> exploring, not a universal law of human nature.
> Please don't enter your workplace dressed as Batman and announce: "I'm
> conducting psychology." Human Resources already has enough to do.

[ENDING]

> So, can Batman make people nicer?
> In this Milan metro study, people were substantially more likely to offer
> their seat when Batman was nearby — even though many helpers claimed they
> never noticed him.
> Maybe superheroes inspire our better nature. Maybe surprise pulls us into the
> present. Or maybe every human being carries a deeply buried fear of
> disappointing Batman.
> Whatever the explanation, the study offers a useful reminder:
> Look up from your phone. Notice the people around you. Offer your seat when
> someone needs it.
> Because kindness shouldn't require a billionaire in tactical pajamas.

[END SCREEN]

> Subscribe for more behavioral science. The statistics suggest Batman would
> want you to.
