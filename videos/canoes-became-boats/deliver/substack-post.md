# Canoes Became Boats

*Where your favourite memory has been.*

[YOUTUBE EMBED: https://youtu.be/HmoptM8k9C0]

Pick the memory you'd save. The afternoon, the kitchen, somebody's face. You've got it, and it's sitting there whole, and you can walk around inside it.

This film follows that memory like a traveller on a voyage - because it has been travelling, and it has come back different, and nobody told you.

Three islands, three real studies:

**Island one, 1932.** Frederic Bartlett gave Cambridge students a Native American folk tale and asked them to tell it back, again and again over months. It drifted. Canoes turned into boats, hunting seals turned into fishing, and the ghosts - which the whole story hangs on - quietly left. You don't keep a copy. You keep a rough shape and rebuild the rest out of whatever's plausible, and plausible means plausible to you.

**Island two, 1974.** Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer showed people a car crash on film. Some were asked how fast the cars were going when they *hit*; others, when they *smashed*. Same film, one different word, different remembered speeds. Your memory is being rebuilt in the room you are standing in, out of the room you are standing in.

**Island three, 2016.** Patrick Nunn and Nicholas Reid gathered Aboriginal Australian stories from twenty-one places around the coast, all describing land that people used to walk across going under the sea - datable to about seven thousand years ago. Scholars of oral tradition will tell you eight hundred years is roughly the limit. The difference is not better memories. It is a structure: one family answerable for a story, other people whose actual job is to hear it and put it right.

And that structure is what Bartlett unknowingly removed. He took a story out of the culture that had been keeping it, handed it to strangers who owed nobody anything, and found that it fell apart. We've spent ninety years quoting that as a fact about the human mind. A good part of it is a fact about the room.

If a memory really matters to you, don't concentrate harder on it. Write it down near the time. Say it out loud to somebody who was there, and let them push back. A memory nobody's allowed to correct isn't a strong one - it is only one that has never been challenged.

And we owe you this at the end: there's a machine telling you all of it, and that machine has no family. So don't let this be the only telling. Go and ask somebody who was there.
