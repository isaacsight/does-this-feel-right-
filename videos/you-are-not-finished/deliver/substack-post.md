# You Are Not Finished

Think of who you were ten years ago.

That comes easily. You can see the clothes. You can hear the song you played until the people around you begged you to stop. You can feel exactly how certain you were about a person, or a job, or a plan that was going to work out.

Now turn around and face the other way. Picture who you will be ten years from now.

Notice what happens. The picture goes soft at the edges. You can manage a rough sketch, but it is mostly this person again, in slightly better shoes, holding slightly more money, with all the same opinions professionally upholstered.

In 2013, three researchers put that question to more than nineteen thousand people, aged eighteen to sixty-eight. They asked one group how much they had changed in the past decade, and another how much they expected to change in the next. Personality. Values. Plain taste — the favourite food, the favourite hobby, the closest friend.

Almost everyone said the same two things. *I have changed a great deal. And I am now, at last, finished.*

The eighteen-year-olds said it. The forty-year-olds said it. The sixty-eight-year-olds said it. Every group could see the road behind them in detail, and every group was confident the road ahead ran straight.

The researchers called it the end of history illusion.

## The part most retellings leave out

The study has problems, and they are worth saying out loud.

It did not follow the same people for ten years; it asked different people of different ages on a single afternoon and lined the answers up. It leaned on memory, and memory flatters — you are the one grading your own past.

And there is a sharper objection. Suppose you know that you will change, but you have no idea which way. What is your best guess for the person you will be? It is the person you are now. Not because you lack imagination, but because that is the smart bet.

So part of this famous effect may be arithmetic rather than blindness.

What survives is smaller and still worth having: change is far easier to see behind you than in front of you.

## The price

The same researchers put a number on the gap. They asked people what they would pay to see their favourite band play in ten years. The answer came back at a hundred and twenty-nine dollars. Then they asked what those same people would pay to see the band they loved ten years ago play tonight. Eighty dollars.

Same money. Same seat. Same band. The only thing that changed was which way you were facing in time.

You will happily buy a ticket for a stranger, as long as the stranger is you.

## A second failure, underneath the first

There is a companion finding by two of the same authors, and it is the one I find harder to shake.

Suppose someone handed you the future — all the events, in order. The job you lose. The person who leaves.

You would still get it wrong. Not the events. The weather. You would badly overestimate how long each one was going to hurt.

Gilbert and Wilson called this the durability bias, and they found it everywhere they looked: a breakup, a rejected job application, an election going the wrong way, a denial of tenure. In every case the forecast was roughly right about the direction and badly wrong about the duration. People expected to be levelled. They were levelled, briefly, and then they were mostly fine.

The reason is almost embarrassing. You have a psychological immune system, and when something bad happens it goes to work immediately, without asking you first. You find the reason it was probably for the best. You quietly rewrite the job you did not get into a job you never wanted. It is not lying, exactly. It is maintenance.

And like your other immune system, you cannot feel it running. So when you picture next year, you picture the wound without the healing. You see yourself standing out in the rain, and you forget that you own a coat, and that you will put it on, and that you will barely remember being wet.

Which is the first mistake again, pointed somewhere new. You are not only bad at picturing who you will be. You are bad at picturing how that person will feel — and you get it wrong in one direction. You underestimate them.

## So

You are not the final draft. The taste you would defend tonight, the plan you are certain of, the person you cannot imagine living without — some of that is scaffolding, and scaffolding comes down.

Treat who you are now as a reasonable working estimate rather than a verdict. Make the promises you can actually keep. Hold the rest a little more loosely.

You are not finished. You are the most recent version, and the next one is already on the way.
