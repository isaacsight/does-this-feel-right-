# CLAIMS — First Come, First Served (the queue)

Every factual claim in the script, with its source. Verified 2026-08-07.

1. **The first written description of a queue is Thomas Carlyle, 1837**, in
   *The French Revolution: A History*, describing Parisians during the bread
   shortages standing in "Queues, or Tails ... so that the first-come be the
   first served." The OED traces the English usage to this text.
   - Carlyle (1837); OED; and the Nineteenth-Century French Studies article
     "'La société en miniature': Queuing at Theatres and Railway Stations in
     Nineteenth-Century Paris" (Taylor & Francis, 2025).

2. **The queue is a product of industrialisation, not of politeness.** Joe
   Moran argues it was an established social form in the early nineteenth
   century, a product of "more urbanised, industrialised societies which
   brought masses of people together in one place." Railways, factory shifts
   and dense cities created the simultaneous crowd; the queue is the thing
   invented to run it.
   - Moran, quoted widely; the same NCFS article on Paris theatres and
     railway stations.

3. **Milgram's queue-intrusion experiment, 1986.** Researchers cut into 129
   waiting lines saying only "Excuse me, I'd like to get in there."
   - Only about **10%** physically prevented the intruder from entering.
   - About **half** reacted — a look, a tut, an eye-roll, a verbal objection —
     and let the intruder stay anyway.
   - With **two** intruders instead of one, objection rose to about **91%**.
   - People standing BEHIND the intrusion point objected more than those in
     front of it, and objection increased with proximity to the cut.
   - Milgram, Liberty, Toledo & Wackenhut (1986), "Response to Intrusion into
     Waiting Lines," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 51(4),
     683-689.

4. **The queue is maintained by shared knowledge rather than authority.**
   Milgram's framing question was whether the line constitutes a social system
   with its own emotional authority — order held by the participants, not by
   anybody in charge.
   - Milgram et al. (1986); Allon & Hanany, "Cutting in Line: Social Norms in
     Queues" (Wharton).

## What this episode does NOT claim

It does not claim the British invented queueing, which is a national
self-image rather than a finding — the first description is French, written by
a Scot. If the script touches British queue-pride it must say so plainly.

It does not put a number on how often queue-jumping succeeds outside
Milgram's conditions, and it does not claim the wartime "waiting your turn as
patriotic duty" framing is a measured effect — that is cultural history, and
the script states it as such or leaves it out.

## Room

**Primary: Larry David** — a man litigating an unwritten rule with a tiny want
and no exit. Line-cutting is the purest form of the Curb grievance.

**Secondary: Michael Schur** — the warm structure. This episode ENDS WELL and
must earn it: three episodes in a row landing on "people are worse than you
think" would make the catalogue one-note. The queue is the case where the
unwritten rule works.

**Structure: Trey Parker** — beats connect with BUT or THEREFORE, never "and
then."

## The spine, and the trap it avoids

The obvious reading of Milgram — people only object when the cut costs them —
is the SAME mechanism as the underdog episode, where sympathy turned out to be
priced. Running it twice would flatten the catalogue. So that finding is a
BEAT, not the thesis.

The thesis: **the queue is the flimsiest enforcement mechanism ever devised,
and it works.** No law, no penalty, no authority. The whole civil order rests
on strangers being willing to make a small disapproving noise, and half of them
will not even do that much — and it still holds.
