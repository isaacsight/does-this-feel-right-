Someone in this house said the shelves would be up by Sunday. It isn't a hard job. Four brackets, a length of pine, and a drill that's been sitting on the garage shelf since the move. It's the kind of promise we make on a Thursday, in a voice with no doubt anywhere in it, because the work has already been done once — done perfectly, in the head, where the pine is straight and the wall has no wiring in it and nobody has to drive back to the hardware store on a hot afternoon for the one bolt the packet didn't include. It's Sunday now. There are no shelves. You've made this promise; we all have — the shed, the spare room, the report that was going to be finished before dinner. And the strange part isn't the delay. The strange part is that next Thursday we'll make the same promise, in the same voice, and mean every word of it.

Because a date, said out loud in that voice, isn't quite a prediction. It works more like a loan. We borrow the hours from a future self — a person who wasn't in the room with us, who didn't sign anything, who has no idea what we've just promised on their behalf — and we spend the borrowed hours immediately. Then our date comes around. And the one who turns up to pay is us.

This has a paper trail, and it's an old one. In 1994, researchers went looking for the cleanest version of the promise they could find, and they found it in honors students — final year, one thesis each, a hard deadline in the spring. They asked one question: when will you finish? The average answer was 33.9 days. The average truth was 55.5. About three in ten finished by the day they'd named for themselves, which means seven in ten were late — people whose entire job that year was one document, with one date on it, and they'd been thinking about it since September. Nobody lied. They pictured the work, pictured themselves doing the work, and said the number that felt honest while they were saying it. You did the same thing this morning, when you looked at something on your list, felt the shape of it, and gave it an afternoon. We all did.

Then the researchers asked a second question, and the second question is what keeps this study alive. Imagine everything going as badly as it possibly could — not the plan, the disaster. So the students sat there and pictured the flu, and the lost draft, and the advisor who goes quiet for five weeks, and out of that private disaster movie they produced a number, and the number was 48.6 days. Reality came in at 55.5. Reality strolled past their worst case and kept walking, by about a week. So what is a worst case actually measuring? When we brace for the worst, we're still standing inside the plan. We just add a cushion. And our cushion is always the wrong size.

There's a second finding buried in the same work, and it's the hopeful one. People who weren't doing the task at all — handed nothing but the person's track record, how the last paper went, how the one before that went — guessed far better than the person holding the calendar. How can that be? You know more about your own project than anybody alive: the plan, the notes, the sore spots, the part you're dreading. And on the single question of when it ends, all of it turns out to be worth less than a short list of the last four times you finished something. That's true of every one of us. Your friends can see you coming. You cannot. Every one of us is somebody else's accurate forecaster, all day, for free.

So there are two ways we can answer the question. The first: look at the job in front of you, break it into steps, imagine each step going the way you mean it to, and add up the pieces. The second: ignore the job almost entirely, and ask what happened the last ten times anybody tried something like this. Researchers call these the inside view and the outside view, and the finding is dull and stubborn. The inside view feels like knowledge. It behaves like hope. And the outside view always feels like cheating, because taking it means refusing to use the one thing you actually have in your hands — the plan, in detail, with your name on it. And we almost never take it.

Now watch the same machine at full size. In 1957 the state of New South Wales priced an opera house for a spit of land in Sydney harbour: seven million dollars, doors open January 1963. Every line of that estimate came from people who built things for a living, in a room, with the drawings on the table. The doors opened in October 1973 — ten years late — at 102 million, which isn't seven million with some slippage. That's a different building. Sorry — the same building. A different number. A child born the year of the estimate could have finished school before the first audience sat down.

The best story about this belongs to the man who named the problem, because it happened to him, with the answer sitting in the room. In 1976, Daniel Kahneman was on a team writing a school curriculum, and one afternoon the team did the pleasant administrative work of guessing when they'd be done. Call it two years — no, call it what the record calls it: eighteen to thirty months. Then one member of that team, a man who had spent his career around projects exactly like this one, was asked what he knew about other teams, and he said, slowly, that he couldn't think of one that had finished in under seven years — and that a fair number had never finished at all. The right number was already in the building. It just wasn't in the plan, and it didn't change the plan. The book took eight years. By then the ministry had lost interest, and it was never used.

Now the obvious objection, and it deserves a hearing: students are students, and a thesis has one author, a soft deadline, and a whole life competing for the hours. Fair. So raise the stakes as high as they go. Across roughly seventy years of records on large public works, nine out of every ten come in over budget — the Channel Tunnel eighty percent over in real terms, Denver's airport two hundred — a pattern so steady the people who study it call it the iron law. These are the most watched, most audited, most expensive plans we know how to make, and they miss in the same direction as a student with one chapter left. And the direction is the whole case. If this were ordinary bad guessing, the errors would fall on both sides of the line, and somewhere there'd be a famous story about the tunnel that opened two years early and cost half what anybody promised — the only story of its kind, told at every dinner in the trade forever. You've never heard that story. Neither has anyone else. Almost nobody overestimates — we all miss in the same direction, together.

In 1979 the rule finally got a name, and the name is a joke on purpose. Hofstadter's Law: it always takes longer than you expect, even when you take into account Hofstadter's Law. It's built to eat its own correction. Add a margin because you know about the law, and the law goes to work on the margin; double your estimate, and you've made a new estimate, the same way you made the first one. Because we do know. Ask anybody naming a date whether the last one ran long and they'll say yes right away, cheerfully, like a joke you both already know the end of — and it changes nothing about the words that come out of our mouths. We take out the loan while looking straight at the last one, and we sign anyway, because the future self is the only one who can cover it, and the only one who isn't in the room to argue.

The shelves went up in March, on a Sunday, four months after the Sunday they were promised for. They hold our books now — slightly too many books, with a lean at the left-hand end the household has agreed not to discuss. None of us remembers the estimate, and nobody is owed a thing. And here's what the research never quite reaches: not one of us would trade the shelves for an accurate forecast about the shelves, because the optimism that ruins the schedule is the same optimism that starts the job. Next Thursday, standing in that same garage, looking at that same wall with that same warm certainty, someone will say the bookcase should take a weekend. We'll believe them. They'll be wrong. And the bookcase, when it finally turns up some time in the autumn, will be beautiful.
