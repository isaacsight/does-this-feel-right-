## The visual anchor — this is the whole fix

The three states are **the same person, in the same chair, in the same room**,
three ways. That image returns every time the film mentions them. Everything
else in the film is built around that one recurring shot. A viewer who loses the
thread can always re-enter on the chair.

## VO — locked

> You've felt this, and probably never had a name for it.
>
> Someone asks you a question in a meeting. You know the answer. And nothing
> comes out.
>
> Not nerves. Not panic. Just nothing.
>
> For about a hundred years, science had two explanations for stress. Fight, or
> run.
>
> But this is neither. You didn't fight. You didn't run. You went still.
>
> And for decades, therapists watched people do exactly this, and had no way to
> explain it.
>
> Then in 1994, a neuroscientist called Stephen Porges suggested something
> simple. There aren't two settings. There are three.
>
> Here's the first. You're with someone you trust. You're relaxed. You're
> talking. Your face moves easily. Porges called this the safe setting — and his
> claim was that it isn't a mood. It's a physical state your body switches into.
>
> Here's the second. Something goes wrong. Heart speeds up, muscles tighten,
> you're ready to argue or ready to leave. That's the one everybody knows. Fight
> or flight.
>
> And here's the third. The one nobody told you about.
>
> You can't fight. You can't leave. So your body does the only thing it has
> left. It shuts down. Your heart slows. You go quiet. You go blank.
>
> Same person. Same chair. Three completely different settings.
>
> And here's why that landed in therapy rooms.
>
> If shutting down is a setting your body switches into, then going blank isn't
> weakness. It isn't you failing to cope. It's the oldest survival move there
> is. Play dead.
>
> So who's doing the switching?
>
> Think about walking into a room where two people have just stopped talking.
> You knew something was wrong before you could say what.
>
> You didn't decide that. Something checked the room and made the call for you.
>
> Porges called that neuroception. And the important part is this: you're not
> doing it. It's being done to you. Constantly. Underneath everything.
>
> One more piece, and it's the one that matters most.
>
> Watch someone pick up a crying baby. The baby stops. Not because of anything
> anyone said. Because of a heartbeat, and a voice.
>
> You never grow out of that. Someone walks into a room and it relaxes. Someone
> else walks in and it doesn't.
>
> Porges called this co-regulation. And his claim is a big one. You are not
> really built to calm yourself down on your own. You're built to be calmed down
> by other people.
>
> Which is worth sitting with — because look at what we've changed.
>
> Every one of those signals is a face, a voice, or a person nearby.
>
> We text instead of calling. We take the meeting with the camera off. We lie in
> bed scrolling, which looks like rest, and settles nothing.
>
> No face. No voice. Nobody calming anybody.
>
> Now, one honest thing.
>
> The biology behind all this is genuinely disputed. In twenty twenty-three, a
> paper went through the theory's five main claims and challenged every one of
> them. Porges has answered. It is not settled.
>
> But here's the thing.
>
> Whether or not the anatomy turns out to be right, the thing it named is real.
> People do go still. People do go blank.
>
> And what it tells you to do — find a face, hear a voice, breathe out slowly,
> be near someone — none of that depends on the biology being correct.
>
> You can doubt the theory and still take the advice.
>
> And maybe that's the useful part. Not that it explains you.
>
> That it stops you being embarrassed about the part of you that goes quiet.
