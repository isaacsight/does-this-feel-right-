## The three rules this rebuild runs on

1. **Relatable setup, impossible payoff.** Every frame is a situation an ordinary
   person has lived through, resolved by a cartoon-physics consequence. A
   recognisable room, then something that cannot happen. Nobody in frame reacts.
2. **Never draw the concept.** No diagrams, no symbol objects, no character
   standing next to a metaphor. Draw the person it is happening to.
3. **Every frame locked to its own line.** No even distribution between anchors —
   that is what produced images landing a beat early for seven minutes.

## Sourcing — unchanged from v1, all verified

Porges introduced the theory in a **1994** address to the Society for
Psychophysiological Research · prior model was two-branch, with freeze described
but unexplained · three states: **ventral vagal** (safety), **sympathetic**
(mobilisation), **dorsal vagal** (shutdown) · **neuroception** (Porges, *Zero to
Three*, 2004) · **co-regulation** · five founding premises challenged —
**Grossman, P. (2023), *Biological Psychology* 180:108589** · phylogenetic claim
contradicted by comparative anatomy · **Porges and others have replied.**

> Say **contested**. Never "debunked" or "pseudoscience". Never imply the therapy
> doesn't help — whether the anatomy is right and whether the practice works are
> different questions, and Act VII turns on that.

> **I — THE GAP**
>
> There's a third thing your body does when it's frightened.
>
> Not fight. Not flight. It just stops.
>
> You know the first two. Heart going, hands cold, ready to swing or ready to
> leave.
>
> That's been the whole story of stress for a hundred years. It was never the
> whole story.
>
> Because there's a third one nobody gave you a word for.
>
> The meeting where you should speak, and don't. The argument where you go
> completely blank. The bad news that lands, and you feel nothing.
>
> Not calm. Empty.
>
> Therapists watched people do this for decades. Described it perfectly.
> Couldn't explain it.
>
> **II — THE PROPOSAL**
>
> Then in 1994, a neuroscientist called Stephen Porges said the map was wrong.
>
> Not two settings. Three.
>
> One for when you're safe — and it doesn't just calm you down, it turns your
> face on. Your voice warms. You can be with people.
>
> One for threat. Heart up, muscles loaded, fight or run.
>
> And one for when you can't do either. When you can't fight it and you can't
> leave.
>
> That one doesn't ease off. It stands on the brake. Everything down. Still.
>
> And here's what made this land in therapy rooms. Shutting down isn't weakness.
> It isn't failing to cope.
>
> It's the oldest trick there is. Play dead.
>
> **III — NEUROCEPTION**
>
> So who's choosing?
>
> Because you don't sit down and pick one.
>
> Porges gave it a name. Neuroception. Not perception — perception is something
> you do. This is done to you.
>
> Underneath awareness, constantly, reading the room. A face. A tone. Something
> behind you.
>
> Picking a setting before you've had an opinion.
>
> It's why you can walk into a room and know something's wrong. Hear four words
> and go on guard.
>
> You weren't being paranoid. You were being informed. Late.

> **IV — CO-REGULATION**

>
> Someone walks in and the room loosens. Someone else walks in and it doesn't.
>
