## The rule this creates for the images

**The narrator states the case. The hero visibly disputes it and is wrong.**
Every frame in the objection beats is a reaction shot: he is mouthing a protest,
holding up a finger, gesturing at himself — while the image proves the narrator
right. That is the engine, and it means the pictures are never decorative.

## VO — locked

> You've felt this. You've probably never had a name for it.
>
> Someone asks you a question in a meeting. You know the answer. And nothing
> comes out.
>
> And you'll tell me that doesn't happen to you. It happened on Tuesday.
>
> For about a hundred years, science had two explanations for stress. Fight, or
> run.
>
> You'll say you do neither, because you stay calm. You didn't stay calm. You
> went still.
>
> You'll call that composure. You went grey and stopped blinking.
>
> Therapists watched people do this for decades and couldn't explain it. Then in
> 1994, a neuroscientist called Stephen Porges suggested something simple. There
> aren't two settings. There are three.
>
> Here's the first. You're with someone you trust. You're relaxed, you're
> talking, your face moves easily. Everyone likes this one.
>
> Here's the second. Something goes wrong. Heart up, muscles tight, ready to
> argue or ready to leave. Everyone knows this one too.
>
> And here's the third. The one you don't want.
>
> You can't fight. You can't leave. So your body does the only thing it has
> left. It shuts down. Your heart slows. You go quiet. You go blank.
>
> Same person. Same chair. Three completely different settings.
>
> And here's why that landed in therapy rooms. If shutting down is a setting
> your body switches into, then going blank isn't weakness. It isn't you failing
> to cope.
>
> It's the oldest survival move there is. Play dead.
>
> And you're going to argue with that. From the floor. Where you are currently
> lying. Thinking.
>
> So who's doing the switching? You'll say you are.
>
> Think about walking into a room where two people have just stopped talking.
> You knew something was wrong before you could say what.
>
> You didn't decide that. Something checked the room and made the call for you.
>
> Porges called it neuroception. You're not doing it. It's being done to you.
> Constantly. Underneath everything.
>
> Most people hate that.
>
> One more piece, and it's the one that matters most.
>
> Watch someone pick up a crying baby. The baby stops. Not because of anything
> anyone said. Because of a heartbeat, and a voice.
>
> You never grow out of that. Someone walks into a room and it relaxes. Someone
> else walks in and it doesn't.
>
> Porges called this co-regulation. You are not really built to calm yourself
> down on your own. You're built to be calmed down by other people.
>

## Shot mix — a bit of everything, with a rule

| Type | Share | For | Test |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Literal** | ~45% | anything the viewer must understand | draw the sentence |
| **Reaction / objection** | ~20% | every "you'll tell me…" beat | he protests, the picture proves him wrong |
| **Cartoon physics** | ~20% | emotional beats | melts, deflates, turns to stone |
| **The chair anchor** | ~10% | the three settings, every mention | same person, same chair, three ways |
| **Absurd flourish** | ~5% | rationed — opossum, lizard | never constant |

**Which one:** *can the viewer be wrong about what they're seeing?* If yes it
must be literal. If the narration has already made the point and they cannot
misread it, that is where the physics goes.
